Claude Fable 5.2 paints images with 1,000-line Python programs, 700,000 strokes, and a deliberate first-line wobble.

Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.2, the frontier variant that shipped November 9 with Mythos 5.2, produces images without a diffusion model or any image encoder. Shown off by Anthropic's Jake Eaton and engineer Michael Graczyk on X, the model writes a Python program, usually between 1,000 and 2,000 lines, that drives a virtual paint engine stroke by stroke. One photorealistic parking garage scene used roughly 700,000 strokes; a sunrise bird painting ran 111 strokes, each a single coordinate line. Fable 5.2 added a small wobble to its first stroke because, in its own reasoning, "a human arm doesn't draw a perfectly straight line either." The model painted over its own mistakes in real time: a late lighting pass that smeared an object was corrected by repainting the object; a thin, scraggly bird was turned back into bolder strokes that read as ruffled feathers.

The Decoder (Manuel Uth) · 2026-11-15 · Source ↗

The Decoder's Manuel Uth put out the fullest write-up on Sunday of a quiet Anthropic launch the company had buried behind the Fable 5.2 pricing announcement a week earlier. Fable 5.2 does not use a diffusion model, does not use an image encoder, and does not call out to a specialist image API. It generates images by writing Python programs, usually between 1,000 and 2,000 lines, that drive a virtual paint engine stroke by stroke. The engine models bristle texture, how paint goes on a canvas, and the fact that there is no undo: mistakes have to be painted over the way a human painter paints over them. Anthropic engineer Jake Eaton and researcher Michael Graczyk posted the demos on X; Uth stitched them together and added benchmark context the X threads had left loose.

The 700,000-stroke parking garage

The flagship demo is a photorealistic parking garage scene composed of roughly 700,000 individual strokes. Each stroke is a small numeric command that chooses a color, a brush, a starting coordinate, a pressure, and a trajectory. The model emits the whole plan as code, runs the code, looks at the result, and then writes more code to correct what it got wrong. A late lighting pass smeared an object in the frame; Fable 5.2 noticed and repainted the object with new highlights. A thin, scraggly bird in a separate painting was turned back into bolder strokes that read as ruffled feathers once the model noticed the shape didn't match the species it had planned. In a shorter demo, a sunrise bird was painted in 111 strokes total, each a single coordinate line in the program listing. The first stroke was wobbled on purpose, the model said in its own trace, because "a human arm doesn't draw a perfectly straight line either."

"A human arm doesn't draw a perfectly straight line either." — Claude Fable 5.2, explaining the wobble on its first stroke, per The Decoder, 2026-11-15

Why painting, not generating

Earlier attempts with the sibling model Opus 5.5 were criticized for drawing too closely from the training distribution and imitating existing art. Fable 5.2's response was to invent artist personas with fictional backstories and consistent styles, and to paint under those personas rather than against a style tag. Anthropic framed the shift at the Fable 5.2 launch on November 9 as a research choice, not a cost choice: Fable 5.2 lists at $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens, down from $10 and $50 on Fable 5.1, but Uth notes the peak per-task cost of $26.50 against $10 for Opus 5.5 at Max tier — paying out more tokens is central to what Fable does. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v5 Fable 5.2 scored 65 at the highest tier, with Gemini 4 Carbon at 60, Opus 5.5 at 59, and Fable 5.1 at about 50. Fermat's Last Theorem was given to roughly 1,000 Fable 5.2 agents in Lean and formalized in 30 hours; an earlier research version had taken eleven days.

"We deliberately limited training gains in capabilities that could most directly accelerate AI development." — Anthropic, on Fable 5.2's recursive-self-improvement caps, per The Decoder, 2026-11-09

The robotics bridge

Uth flags the painting work as evidence of something Anthropic is not saying out loud. If a language model can plan and self-correct across hundreds of thousands of constrained, ordered actions, the same primitive looks applicable to physical robotics. Anthropic's internal RoboDojo Sim benchmark bears that out on paper: Fable 5.2 scored 64.9 percent against 29.8 percent for OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra. In a real-world robot-arm test run by researcher Aris Zhu, both models were asked to paint Van Gogh's The Starry Night; Astra painted faster and was the only one of the two to stand a domino on end, while Fable 5.2 showed weaker fine motor control. The simulation-to-real gap is still there. Uth also surfaces Anthropic's internal claims — unverifiable from outside — that Fable 5.2 solved two open problems in black-hole physics, designed three working electronic devices with Diode, and ran more than a thousand sub-agents to program a laptop operating system in eight days.

"Pixels and joint commands as a shared denoising target." — Runway Research page on Praxis-1, 2026-11-14; similar logic as Fable 5.2's paint engine

What it means for image generation

The practical read is that diffusion is not the only route to a general-purpose image model, and it may not be the most steerable one. Painting through code opens the door to editing an image the way one edits a program: find the lines that drew the misshapen bird, rewrite them. Midjourney and Stable Diffusion cannot be edited that way without a mask. The constraint is throughput: 700,000 strokes is a lot of tokens, and Anthropic has not published a cost-per-image for the parking garage. The IPO roadshow runs through this week; Anthropic is scheduled to price ANT at $70 to $78 per share on November 18 and start trading November 19 on Nasdaq. Uth frames the Sunday demos as Anthropic's weekend assist for the roadshow, not a product launch.

Meituan opens LongCat-2.5 1.6T MoE agent model under MIT, self-hosting guide names 194 Huawei Ascend accelerators and no Nvidia GPU.

The Decoder (Jonathan Kemper) · 2026-11-15 · Source ↗

LongCat-2.5 ships MIT-licensed weights on Hugging Face plus a separate W4A4 version that targets Chinese chips natively; Meituan's self-hosting guide has no Nvidia GPU guidance anywhere. The sample deployment runs on 194 Huawei Ascend Atlas A2 accelerators: two for images, 64 for prompt ingestion, 128 for response generation. Terminal Bench v2.1 posted 88.4 (Opus 5 is at 89.1, GPT-5.6 Sol 88.8); DeepSWE jumped to 64.6 from 9.7 for LongCat-2.0. Jonathan Kemper's Sunday write-up in The Decoder lands one week after Meituan unveiled LongCat-2.5 and now puts the weights, the quantized variant, and the self-hosting path in public hands. LongCat-2.5 is a 1.6-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model, with about 48 billion active parameters per token, a 1-million-token input context, a 128,000-token output limit over the API, and reasoning that can be toggled on and off. It ingests text, images, and video and emits text; images are converted into tokens at native resolution rather than through a separate encoder. The model inherits from LongCat-2.0 and from the earlier research model LongCat-Next, and is explicitly positioned for long, multi-step agent work, coding above all.

Trained on Chinese hardware, deployed on Huawei Ascend

Meituan describes LongCat-2.5 as trained "entirely on Chinese hardware" without naming the training cluster. Kemper flags LongCat-2.0's prior disclosure — more than 50,000 Chinese AI chips in late June — as the closest public reference. The reinforcement-learning pipeline runs tasks built from real GitHub pull requests and recreations of past agent failures; training environments block internet access and strip Git history and cached files so the model can't cheat by remembering the answer, and an LLM checks solutions for shortcuts. Capabilities were distilled from several specialized teacher models covering agent work, reasoning, and dialog.

The deployment side is where the Nvidia story sits. Meituan's self-hosting guide, built on top of the SGLang-FluentLLM NPU runtime, names 194 Huawei Ascend Atlas A2 accelerators in its sample configuration: two accelerators for image processing, 64 for prompt ingestion, 128 for response generation. The W4A4 quantization — 4-bit weights, 4-bit activations — is native on Chinese chips; against the 8-bit baseline, Meituan's own load tests show prompt processing up 43.3 percent and response generation up 42.77 percent while accuracy stays "on par with the uncompressed model."

"On par with the uncompressed model." — Meituan, on the W4A4 quantization in LongCat-2.5, per The Decoder, 2026-11-15

Benchmarks and the DeepSWE jump

The Terminal Bench v2.1 score of 88.4 sits beside Claude Opus 5 at 89.1, GPT-5.6 Sol at 88.8, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash at the table top of 90.6. On DeepSWE LongCat-2.5 jumped from 9.7 on LongCat-2.0 to 64.6, behind DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash at 74.2 and GPT-5.6 Sol at 72.7 but ahead of Claude Opus 4.8 in the published table. BrowseComp topped the chart at 91.9 but was run in Meituan's own environment. Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol are not in the DeepSWE comparison, a conspicuous absence.

Distribution

Weights are MIT-licensed, allowing commercial use, modification, and redistribution. A separate W4A4 variant is also published. The chat endpoint lives at longcat.ai; the API supports both OpenAI and Anthropic request formats and works with agent harnesses including Claude Code, Codex, and Hermes. Launch promotional pricing is about €0.27 per million input tokens and €1.07 per million output tokens — a steep discount against Western frontier models. LongCat-2.0 ran anonymously as "Owl Alpha" on OpenRouter before its public release, which Kemper notes as a pattern for how Chinese labs are now timing the open-weight drop.

"The self-hosting guide has no Nvidia GPU guidance anywhere." — The Decoder, paraphrasing Meituan's documentation, 2026-11-15

VentureBeat: Palo Alto Unit 42 measures 78.3% attack success on agents connected to five MCP servers once one server is compromised.

VentureBeat (Nilay Dhamsania, Intuz) · 2026-11-15 · Source ↗

Intuz AI strategist Nilay Dhamsania wrote that Palo Alto Networks Unit 42 found a 78.3 percent attack success rate when five MCP servers were connected to a single agent and one was compromised, with instructions riding cascading trust into every subsequent tool call. OWASP's MCP Top 10 says only two of ten risk categories sit in the text the agent reads; the other eight live in infrastructure the protocol doesn't constrain. MCP launched in November 2024 with no authorization framework; revisions in March, June, November 2025 and July 2026 added OAuth and deprecated Dynamic Client Registration.

VentureBeat ran Nilay Dhamsania's Sunday analysis as the orchestration beat's main security piece. Dhamsania is an AI strategist and director at Intuz; the piece is a practitioner argument, not a vendor announcement. The argument is simple: a single compromised MCP server can inject instructions that spread through "cascading trust" into every subsequent tool call an agent makes. Once an agent treats one server's output as trustworthy, those instructions carry into calls to other servers, including servers that were never compromised. The example Dhamsania works through is a help-desk agent given access to a mail store, a calendar, a ticket system, and an internal wiki; a prompt injection in one of those surfaces can reach all four.

The 78.3 percent number

Palo Alto Networks Unit 42 ran testing with five MCP servers connected to a single agent. Once Unit 42 compromised a single server, attacks succeeded at a 78.3 percent rate across the agent's full tool set. Dhamsania maps the result to OWASP's MCP Top 10 taxonomy: ten risk categories, of which only two are visible to the agent itself as text. The other eight — credential handling, scope boundaries, identity, token lifetime, consent flow, server-to-server authentication, logging, and rotation — sit in infrastructure the MCP protocol doesn't constrain. The NSA's May 2026 guidance for agentic deployments, mapped by Equixly to the OWASP list, flagged the same split.

"It never standardized how you prove who's calling or what they're allowed to touch." — Nilay Dhamsania, Intuz, on MCP's original 2024 spec, in VentureBeat, 2026-11-15

The protocol timeline

MCP launched in November 2024 with no authorization framework. The March 2025 revision added OAuth-based controls; the June 2025 revision strengthened session management; the November 2025 revision tightened tool-call scoping; the July 2026 revision deprecated Dynamic Client Registration (DCR), which Dhamsania frames as a response to the "rug pull" pattern in which a trusted dependency silently changes behavior after registration. The piece calls out hard-coded credentials and token mismanagement as the two most common Unit 42 findings in the field — tokens scoped to full mailbox access where read-only would do, as Microsoft Azure guidance put it in its own write-up that Dhamsania cites.

"A compromised server can inject instructions that ride along into every subsequent tool call." — Nilay Dhamsania, VentureBeat, 2026-11-15

What Microsoft's internal deployments say

Microsoft's Inside Track blog and Azure security guidance, Dhamsania writes, treat MCP deployment as external by default: "internal isn't the same as trusted," the Microsoft text reads, as summarized in the article. The practical takeaway Dhamsania pulls out is three-part: scope tokens tightly; log and attribute every tool call to a human or service identity; and pin MCP server versions so a July rug pull can't re-enter on the next run.

"Internal isn't the same as trusted." — Microsoft Inside Track, cited by Dhamsania, 2026-11-15

The deployment baseline

Gartner forecasts 40 percent of enterprise applications will include task-specific AI agents by the end of 2026. Combine that with the Unit 42 cascade number and the OWASP mapping and the piece's argument writes itself: the agentic layer is going into production without a standard identity layer, and the incident curve has to flatten through convention. The deprecation of DCR in July is the first standards-level response; Dhamsania argues it isn't enough without enterprise policy that treats MCP tool chains as mutually untrusted by default.

Manuel Uth's Sunday Decoder write-up walks through the Reserve Bank of Australia's latest labor-market monitoring report, "Monitoring early effects of AI on the labour market," by RBA researcher Mike Major. The headline finding is a careful one: AI has barely displaced any jobs in Australia so far, Major writes, but the youngest workers in the most AI-exposed occupations have measurably lost ground. The monitoring system focuses on entry-level workers and highly exposed occupations — programming, clerical work, and customer service. Job postings have fallen across all exposure groups since ChatGPT's late-2022 launch, with the steepest drop in the highest-exposure group.

"AI has barely displaced any jobs in Australia so far." — Mike Major, Reserve Bank of Australia, "Monitoring early effects of AI on the labour market," cited in The Decoder, 2026-11-15

The young-worker gap

In the highest-exposure quintile, workers aged 15 to 24 and recent college graduates have weaker employment rates and higher unemployment than older workers in the same quintile. Employment among younger people in the high-exposure group has declined since late 2022, while employment among older workers in the same group has risen. Major is careful on causation: theoretical exposure is not the same as actual corporate AI use, and the post-pandemic business cycle, remote-work normalization, and other macro factors could carry the signal. SEEK data referenced in the report suggests AI is complementing existing work in many workflows and generating demand for AI-related skills at the same time.

Studies in context

Uth sets the RBA finding alongside the recent US and European literature. Stanford's August 2025 US payroll analysis showed a roughly 13 percent drop in relative employment for 22-to-25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations since late 2022, and nearly 20 percent for young software developers. Anthropic's March 2026 study found job-finding rates for 22-to-25-year-olds in exposed occupations fell about 14 percent, with no systematic rise in overall unemployment. Anthropic's internal review showed its own junior-technical hire share fell from 27 percent to 9 percent since early 2025; in August 2026, Anthropic's AI agents logged six times as many hours as human employees. Denmark's 25,000-worker study found no measurable changes in wages or hours despite a 64 percent AI adoption rate, with workers saving an average of 2.8 percent of their working time.

"77 percent of surveyed companies prioritize upskilling over job cuts." — WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025, as cited by Uth, 2026-11-15

The LinkedIn counterweight

A January 2026 LinkedIn/US unemployment analysis found problems in exposed occupations began in early 2022, before ChatGPT's launch, suggesting macroeconomic factors may matter more than AI alone. LinkedIn also reported AI-related job postings grew sixfold in one year. WEF's Future of Jobs 2025 projected a net gain of 78 million jobs by 2030 and showed 77 percent of surveyed companies prioritize upskilling over job cuts.
