Four Trillium TPUs make orbit. Radiators now decide whether Suncatcher works.

Google confirmed telemetry at 650 kilometers from the Minimum Viable Prototype carrying four Trillium-generation Cloud TPUs, which rode Transporter-18 off Vandenberg on October 1 and radio-checked that evening. Oct 2 reporting shifted the technical narrative off radiation and onto heat. Each chip needs roughly 1.3 square meters of radiator surface to dump the thermal load; the vacuum will not carry heat away by convection, so the mission runs 15-minute compute bursts and then cools. UC Davis's Crocker Nuclear Laboratory had already cleared the Trillium silicon for 15 krad against an expected 750 rad across five years. Travis Beals, Google's Senior Director for the project, said the hardware is "operating as expected."

Tech · Techtimes · 2026-10-02 · Source ↗

Google's MVP satellite rode Transporter-18 off Vandenberg Space Force Base at 2:32 p.m. Eastern on October 1 and radio-checked that evening at an altitude of roughly 650 kilometers, a dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit that keeps the solar panels illuminated most of the time. Four Trillium-generation Cloud TPUs are on board, operating on about a kilowatt from the Planet Labs-built spacecraft bus. The spacecraft and payload together are compute-equivalent to a single data-center server. By the morning of October 2, Techtimes had reframed the whole mission around a problem that was second-order in Google's November 2025 announcement and now sits in front of everything else: heat.

The vacuum does not carry heat away by convection. The only path out is radiation. Google's own thermal brief says it needs roughly 1.3 square meters of radiator surface area per TPU to dissipate the generated load, with heat pipes moving the energy from the die to external radiators that reject it as infrared. The amount of power a radiator can shed scales with surface area and the fourth power of its temperature, by Stefan-Boltzmann; a radiator that gets hotter can reject more energy, but only up to the limit at which the thermal boundary layer between die and radiator breaks down. On this prototype the operational duty cycle is fifteen minutes of compute followed by a cooling pause. The satellite also sees a temperature swing above 100 degrees Celsius between sunlit and shadowed sides as it crosses the terminator. The pass crossing matters: a dawn-dusk SSO means the orbit tracks the terminator and the thermal gradient is persistent, not periodic.

"Operating as expected." — Travis Beals, Senior Director, Google Project Suncatcher

Radiation retired as the question

Pre-launch radiation testing at UC Davis's Crocker Nuclear Laboratory used a 67 MeV proton beam against AI workloads running on actual Trillium silicon. The TPUs cleared 15 krad total ionizing dose. Google projects roughly 750 rad(Si) accumulated across the five-year planned mission envelope, with HBM irregularities starting to show above 2 krad(Si). One silent data corruption event occurred during the beam campaign; Google characterized it as within tolerance. The headline of Techtimes's follow-up is that the radiation question is retired for this hardware generation and the mission is now about thermal engineering, not nuclear physics.

What this is a test of

Project Suncatcher is Google's long-term bet that solar-powered compute at scale can live in orbit rather than in Nebraska. The MVP will not demonstrate inter-satellite laser linking, scale economics, or launch-cost trajectory. Two prototypes built with Planet Labs are slated to launch by early 2027. Operational life is targeted at approximately one year, with up to six years before atmospheric reentry; solar-panel degradation is projected at 0.5 to 0.8 percent per year. For orbital compute to pencil out at scale, Google says the industry needs launch cost below $200 per kilogram by the mid-2030s, which requires roughly 1,800 Starship launches over 10 years at a 200-metric-ton-per-launch payload.

The crowded orbit

The competitive field is already dense. Starcloud deployed an Nvidia H100 to orbit in November 2025 and ran the first orbital LLM training in March 2026, closing a $170 million Series A at a $1.1 billion valuation. SpaceX's Starmind AI1 prototype plans 72 Rubin GPUs and 36 Vera CPUs per satellite, with 150 kilowatts sustained, targeting early 2027. Axiom Space placed two data-center nodes in orbit in January 2026. Blue Origin's TeraWave constellation is scoped at roughly 5,400 satellites. China announced a 200,000-satellite constellation in January 2026. Neil deGrasse Tyson, who called orbital data centers "a failed business model," is on record as a skeptic. Google's reply is on-orbit telemetry, which it now has.

Ai2 open-sources AstaBrief 8B. One pass, 51 seconds, 90.5 citation precision.

Unite.AI · 2026-10-02 · Source ↗

The Allen Institute released AstaBrief 8B on Hugging Face under Apache 2.0, with weights, training data, and example workflows. The model is a Qwen3-8B supervised fine-tune with DPO on top, trained on 47,000 filtered research questions plus 6,000 preference pairs, and writes a cited report in a single pass. Average time per report is 51.1 seconds, 3.5x faster than Ai2's prior Claude-backed Thinking mode at 178.5 seconds, with 90.5 citation precision and 78.2 recall against ScholarQA-CS2.

Ai2 shipped AstaBrief 8B to Hugging Face Friday morning as the Fast mode inside the Allen Institute's Asta agentic platform. The license is Apache 2.0, the base model is Qwen3-8B, and the full training data is open along with the weights and example workflows. The model takes a research question and a set of retrieved literature excerpts and returns a cited research report. The pitch is that the whole report is written in one pass instead of the Claude-powered pipeline's chunked multi-stage summarization, and that the architectural simplification is the main source of the speed-up.

The headline comparison numbers are 51.1 seconds average time per report versus 178.5 seconds for Thinking mode (Claude-powered) on the same input corpus, which Ai2 characterizes as about 3.5x faster. On ScholarQA-CS2 the model records 87 across tracked metrics overall, 90.5 citation precision, 78.2 citation recall, 89 answer precision, and an LLM-judged win rate of 55 percent on the development split and 72 percent on the held-out test split. The training set is 90,000 research-focused queries filtered for quality and privacy; 47,000 cleared the supervised stage; roughly 6,000 preference pairs drove the DPO stage. Ai2 said it tested reinforcement-learning-based training and chose the simpler SFT + DPO recipe because RL was unstable and expensive on this task.

"For research and educational use under Ai2's Responsible Use Guidelines." — AstaBrief 8B license and intended-use statement, Ai2

Open weights behind the firewall

The deployment angle Ai2 pressed is that institutions can run the model on their own hardware, inside their own firewalls, when a research question involves unpublished work. That is the use-case Asta's Thinking mode cannot serve because routing to Claude leaves the enterprise VPC. For a research group writing cited literature reviews or drafting method sections, the 90.5 citation precision figure is the critical quality metric: an open model that costs the price of GPU time per report and keeps citation accuracy above 90 percent against the ScholarQA-CS2 baseline begins to displace the proprietary pipeline for the bulk of the work. Ai2 kept the Claude Thinking mode available for queries that still need deeper reasoning.

The timing matters because AstaBrief lands into the open-weights slice where Qwen 3-level models trained on narrow research-report objectives have been the hottest area of academic deployment through Q3. Jonas Reeve at Unite.AI wrote up the release as the clearest demonstration this year that a small open model with a well-chosen objective can match or beat a general-purpose large model on a task-specific workflow at a fraction of the serving cost.

JEDEC publishes the first silicon-photonics reliability standard.

HPCwire · 2026-10-02 · Source ↗

JEDEC Solid State Technology Association released JESD264: Silicon Photonics Qualification and Reliability Requirements, the first industry-wide standard for optical device qualification. The spec covers modulators, photodiodes, waveguides, assembly, and laser integration, and names reliability ownership between foundries, OSATs, and system developers. Without such a baseline, deployments have carried a chain-of-responsibility gap that slowed enterprise adoption at a moment when optical interconnects are being asked to carry gigawatt-scale AI traffic.

JEDEC published JESD264 Friday as the first industry-wide reliability standard for silicon photonics. The scope covers component-level qualification for the full stack of optical devices used in AI, datacenter, and telecom infrastructure: modulators, photodiodes, waveguides, laser integration, and assembly. The document also sets responsibility boundaries between foundries, OSATs (outsourced semiconductor assembly and test), and system developers, which until now have operated without a shared framework for who owns which failure modes. Silicon photonics has lacked a counterpart to the common CMOS reliability regime; JESD264 fills that gap.

The standard arrives in the middle of silicon photonics' transition from a long-promised research technology to a load-bearing piece of hyperscale AI infrastructure. Optical interconnects now carry a growing share of accelerator-to-accelerator traffic at the gigawatt scale, where copper's power-per-bit cost has become prohibitive. Both pluggable optics vendors and co-packaged optics suppliers have cited reliability uncertainty as a structural brake on volume deployment. The JEDEC spec's prescriptions for assembly processes and laser integration are particularly aimed at co-packaged optics, where thermal cycling and mechanical stress near the die have introduced new reliability questions that pluggable transceivers never faced.

Supply-chain contract

The responsibility clauses are the piece of JESD264 that observers inside semi are watching most closely. Silicon photonics devices today pass through several ownership transitions: silicon fabrication at the foundry, laser-die integration, package assembly at an OSAT, module integration at a system developer, and installation at a hyperscaler. When a device fails at month 36, the chain of liability has typically broken down in the middle. JESD264 names which qualification responsibilities land at which stage. Foundries own die-level characterization, OSATs own package qualification, and system developers own module and integration qualification. The hpcwire write-up calls the ownership delineation the standard's "most practical contribution," which is a fair characterization — standards without clear ownership rarely survive the first failure analysis.

The technology CScale is building is integrated optical light-engine hardware for AI scale-up: the inside-the-pod bandwidth that lets thousands of accelerators behave as a single very large computer. Scale-up is distinct from scale-out, which is the lower-bandwidth, higher-latency inter-pod traffic that today's data-center fabrics already handle. In the gigawatt-class AI data centers expected by the end of the decade, a single scale-up domain will span thousands of accelerators across dozens of racks, demanding bandwidth measured in petabits per second with predictable low latency. Copper runs out of power budget below that scale; optics have the throughput but have struggled with reliability under individual-link failure.

Graceful degradation

CScale's distinguishing architectural choice is that an optical light engine fails gracefully rather than taking down the whole scale-up domain. Current designs often tie many lanes together through shared laser sources; a single laser failure can propagate into a wide outage. CScale's optics let individual links fail without interrupting the accelerators sharing a pod — the kind of containment property that networking vendors have been chasing for a decade. The company has not published performance numbers but the Nvidia strategic participation plus the Series C size suggests performance validation is already in Nvidia's hands.

Branch Target Reuse pulls a Linux root hash through BPF JIT in minutes.

SecurityWeek · 2026-10-02 · Source ↗

VUSec and Scuola Superiore Sant'Anna disclosed Branch Target Reuse on Oct 2, a new Spectre-v2 variant that abuses stale branch predictions against just-in-time compilers used by kernels, browsers and runtimes. The VUSec end-to-end exploit targets Linux's classic BPF JIT and leaks arbitrary memory at eight bytes per second, including a root password hash out of a fully patched Intel machine with Spectre defenses enabled, in three to five minutes. CVE-2026-64507 and CVE-2026-64508 cover the kernel side; patches have landed. Intel, AMD and Arm are all affected.

Researchers at VUSec (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam) and Scuola Superiore Sant'Anna published Branch Target Reuse (BTR) Friday as the first Spectre-v2 variant that targets just-in-time compilers specifically. The target surface — JIT compilers used in operating-system kernels, web browsers and language runtimes — is one of the most load-bearing pieces of modern software, and the attack class turns the JIT itself into a Spectre-v2 launchpad rather than requiring attacker-controlled branches already in the victim. All processors the VUSec team evaluated were vulnerable, across Intel, AMD, and Arm.

The end-to-end exploit the paper demonstrates runs against the Linux kernel's classic BPF JIT. On a fully patched Intel machine with default Spectre defenses enabled, the exploit pulls the root password hash out of kernel memory in three to five minutes, exfiltrating arbitrary memory at roughly eight bytes per second. The two CVEs filed on the kernel side, CVE-2026-64507 and CVE-2026-64508, carry fixes that have already landed; the Linux kernel added Indirect Branch Predictor Barrier (IBPB) flush on BPF JIT allocations earlier in the year, which forms part of the defense in depth. The researchers' point is that the architectural root cause — speculative execution reusing branch-target entries across JIT allocations — remains, and that similar attack patterns are likely to recur in browser JavaScript JITs and Java/.NET runtimes without analogous mitigations.

"The classic BPF JIT in a fully patched Intel system, default protections on, yields the root password hash in three to five minutes." — VUSec + SSSA, BTR disclosure

Why it matters now

JIT compilers are everywhere a workload needs runtime code generation, which includes most of what any modern AI stack does for operator fusion and dynamic dispatch. The disclosure arrives alongside a visible shift in how hyperscalers are thinking about Spectre-class mitigations in multi-tenant AI compute: pre-generation code caches and ahead-of-time compilation reduce the JIT surface but add latency to inference-serving stacks that have been obsessively latency-tuned. VUSec's recommended mitigations keep IBPB on JIT writes mandatory and add JIT-spraying hardening in the kernel's BPF path.
