Munich court rules Suno infringed six GEMA copyrights, the first landmark European verdict against a generative-AI vendor

The Munich Regional Court's 42nd Civil Chamber ruled Friday that Suno's v3.5 and v4 music-generation models memorized and reproduced six GEMA-represented songs including 'Forever Young,' 'Daddy Cool,' 'Rasputin' and 'Mambo No. 5' from a corpus of more than two million tracks scraped by circumventing YouTube's 'rolling cipher' protection. Judge Elke Schwager granted injunctive relief, ordered disclosure of illicit revenue, and set damages to be quantified. GEMA CEO Tobias Holzmüller called the verdict "of global significance"; Suno said it disagrees and will "evaluate all available options, including an appeal." The ruling lands 36 hours before the EU AI Act's GPAI enforcement powers activate at 00:00 UTC on August 2.

Musically · 2026-07-31 · Source ↗

The Munich Regional Court's 42nd Civil Chamber ruled Friday that Suno, the US AI music-generation startup, infringed the reproduction and public-communication rights held by German collecting society GEMA on six German-language and German-repertoire tracks, granting injunctive relief and ordering Suno to disclose the revenue it derived from the infringement. Presiding judge Elke Schwager found that Suno's v3.5 and v4 models memorized and reproduced 'Forever Young' by Alphaville, 'Daddy Cool' by Boney M., 'Rasputin' by Boney M., 'Mambo No. 5' by Lou Bega, 'Bochum' by Herbert Grönemeyer and 'Über den Wolken' by Reinhard Mey. Evidence introduced at trial showed the models were trained on a corpus of more than two million songs, the majority obtained by 'stream-ripping' YouTube — a method that circumvents YouTube's proprietary 'rolling cipher' content-protection scheme. The court held that both the training pass (a reproduction) and the model's ability to generate outputs closely resembling the works (a public communication) constitute distinct copyright violations under German and US law. Damages will be quantified after Suno discloses the requested revenue, which the court framed as revenue earned during the period of infringing training and inference.

"This is a verdict of global significance. AI providers must acquire licenses and may not use the works of our members free of charge. AI models based on the theft of intellectual property are not protected by law."— Tobias Holzmüller, GEMA CEO

What Suno's defense argued and what the court rejected

Suno's defense had leaned on two arguments. First, that training on copyrighted material in the United States falls within US fair-use precedent and therefore could not be sanctioned by a German court. Second, that the model does not store copies of the training data but instead learns statistical patterns, and that any resemblance in output to a specific song is a coincidence of the generative process rather than reproduction. The Munich chamber rejected both. On the fair-use argument the court held that when a model trained in the US is subsequently deployed to generate outputs accessible to German users on European services, the reproduction and communication rights under German copyright law are triggered inside Germany, independent of the training location. On the memorization argument the court accepted expert evidence that the six works could be reproduced with a level of fidelity — including recognizable melodic phrases, chord progressions and lyric fragments — sufficient to constitute copies for legal purposes, and that the model's ability to produce those outputs on demand is itself evidence that the training set was reproduced in the model weights. The court also credited GEMA's evidence that Suno's scraping used the YouTube 'rolling cipher' circumvention route, which independently violates the anti-circumvention provisions of the InfoSoc Directive.

Damages, disclosure, and the appeal path

The ruling issued Friday is not final. Suno's US spokesperson said the company "disagrees" with the decision, calling it "a fundamental mischaracterization of the technology," and confirmed the company is "evaluating all available options, including an appeal" to the Munich Higher Regional Court (OLG München). Any appeal will not stay the injunctive relief, which forbids Suno from using the six works for further training or inference into Europe. The disclosure order carries teeth: GEMA can now calculate damages as a share of Suno's global revenue derived from the infringing model versions during the period 2023 through 2026, a figure the plaintiff's team has previewed as running into eight or nine figures depending on how the accounting treats subscription revenue that spans infringing and non-infringing outputs. Damages are the standard measure under German law and follow the "license analogy," i.e., what Suno would have paid GEMA had it acquired a license upfront.

Why it matters — and why it lands now

The GEMA ruling is the first landmark European copyright verdict against a generative-AI vendor. It arrives 36 hours before the EU AI Act's General-Purpose AI enforcement machinery activates at 00:00 UTC on August 2, giving the European Commission and the AI Office authority to demand training-data documentation, run technical evaluations of models, restrict or withdraw a model from the EU market, and issue fines up to €15 million or 3 percent of global turnover under Article 101. The ruling maps directly onto the compliance surface that August 2 activates: the AI Act's Copyright chapter requires GPAI providers to publish a "sufficiently detailed summary" of training data and to maintain a documented copyright compliance policy. Any provider that trained on German-repertoire music without licensing now sits in the same evidentiary posture as Suno. GEMA has parallel proceedings on file against a second generative-music vendor in Munich; other European collecting societies including SACEM (France) and SIAE (Italy) have been assembling similar cases through the spring. The Munich chamber's willingness to attribute reproduction to model weights themselves — rather than only to the training pass — is the doctrinal move rights-holders' counsel had been waiting for.

"Today's decision fundamentally mischaracterizes how our technology works. We will evaluate all available options, including an appeal."— Suno, Inc. statement

Trump executive order hits Aug 1 deadline for voluntary Frontier AI Framework as Altman meets Wiles, Cairncross, Kratsios and Lutnick

CNBC / Unite.AI · 2026-07-30 · Source ↗

Executive Order 14409 gave Treasury, the War Department (via NSA) and DHS (via CISA) 60 days to design a testing regime for frontier AI. The voluntary framework narrows pre-release model review to 30 days from 90 in the earlier draft and prohibits mandatory licensing.

The August 1 deliverables deadline in President Trump's June 2 executive order "Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security" (EO 14409) arrived Saturday with the voluntary Frontier AI Framework still moving through final White House review. The EO directed the Treasury Department, the War Department (through the National Security Agency) and the Department of Homeland Security (through the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency) to build a classified benchmarking regime capable of identifying AI models with advanced cyber capabilities, and gave those agencies 60 days to publish a voluntary framework under which frontier developers can engage the federal government prior to public release. The draft that circulated to OpenAI, Anthropic and Google in mid-July narrows the pre-release review window to 30 days, down from the 90 days envisioned in an earlier draft, and explicitly prohibits "a mandatory governmental licensing, preclearance, or permitting requirement" for developing, publishing, releasing or distributing new models.

"I have seen the proposed framework for implementing the executive order and I certainly support AI cybersecurity guardrail legislation."— Sam Altman, OpenAI CEO, to CNBC

Sam Altman met Wednesday with White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles, National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross, White House Science and Technology Adviser Michael Kratsios and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to preview the framework and discuss OpenAI's participation. Altman told CNBC he "certainly" supports AI cybersecurity guardrail legislation but did not commit OpenAI to a mandatory pre-release vetting process. Two questions remain unresolved on the eve of the deadline: how the framework defines the class of "covered frontier models," and whether open-weight releases (which cannot be recalled) will be treated on the same review timeline as closed API releases. The framework does not have the force of law; its instrument is the 30-day voluntary window in which the federal government can review a new frontier model's national-security implications before public release, with unstated consequences if a developer opts out. The White House had not published the framework document as of Saturday morning UTC; its release is expected in the coming days.

The launch carries safety-policy weight the visible image watermarking did not. Voice cloning and audio deepfakes have driven regulatory attention through the year: the FCC put deepfake voice calls under existing telemarketing law in January 2024, the EU AI Act's Article 50 transparency obligations for AI-generated audio activate August 2, and several US state laws (California, Minnesota, Texas) require disclosure on AI-generated political audio. OpenAI's move puts a machine-readable provenance signal on the largest voice-generation surface in production. One day remains until EU AI Act GPAI enforcement powers activate at 00:00 UTC August 2

European Commission · 2026-07-31 · Source ↗

Article 101 fine authority for General-Purpose AI providers reaches up to €15 million or 3 percent of global turnover; Article 99 broader AI Act violations reach €35 million or 7 percent. Obligations applied from August 2, 2025; enforcement machinery held back one year to allow provider and AI Office readiness.

The EU AI Act's General-Purpose AI enforcement machinery activates at 00:00 UTC Sunday August 2, giving the European Commission and the AI Office authority to request GPAI documentation, run technical evaluations of models, demand risk-mitigation and compliance measures, restrict or withdraw a model from the EU market, and issue fines. Under Article 101 the ceiling for GPAI-specific violations reaches €15 million or 3 percent of global annual turnover, whichever is higher. Under Article 99, broader AI Act breaches reach €35 million or 7 percent. The GPAI obligations themselves — technical documentation for downstream integrators, a publicly available training-data summary, a copyright compliance policy, and (for models meeting systemic-risk thresholds) additional safety and cybersecurity obligations — have applied from August 2, 2025; the enforcement machinery was held back one year to give both providers and the AI Office time to operationalize.

"August 2, 2026 is when the AI Act's General-Purpose AI provisions become enforceable in practice. The compliance surface has been visible for a year; the sanction surface starts tomorrow."— European Commission AI Office FAQ, updated 2026-07-31

Compliance posture across the frontier labs is uneven on the eve of the activation. OpenAI's Friday "Advancing Responsible AI Across Europe" statement addressed the Transparency and Safety chapters but explicitly omitted the Copyright chapter. Anthropic and Google DeepMind have both signed the GPAI Code of Practice under the voluntary self-certification path but have not published complete training-data summaries. Meta has not signed the Code. Under the Act, non-signatories to the voluntary Code face the same substantive obligations but with a higher default evidentiary burden if the AI Office opens an inquiry. The Munich Regional Court's GEMA v. Suno verdict issued Friday maps directly onto the compliance surface that activates Sunday and gives European rights-holders a national-court precedent for reproduction-and-communication claims independent of the AI Act's own enforcement mechanism.
