Policy · ABC News / Fortune / The Next Web · 2026-09-23 · Source ↗

The UN Security Council convened its first standing session on frontier artificial intelligence on Wednesday afternoon, September 23, at UN Headquarters in New York, with Sam Altman and Dario Amodei giving oral briefings to the fifteen member states alongside Hugging Face CEO Clem Delangue and Turing Award laureate Yoshua Bengio, who co-chairs the UN AI Advisory Body. France, holding the September Council presidency, called the meeting. It came one day after Dario Amodei published a New York Times op-ed warning that autonomous AI systems already exceed human ability on many economically consequential tasks and that "the challenge is bigger than any one company."

Altman framed the moment as a fork in the road. "We have a choice in front of us. AI can either be more like a new renaissance of creativity and discovery, or more like a new industrial revolution of upheaval and disarray," he told the Council. He called for internationally agreed standards on the capability testing of frontier models, "accurate and speedy" incident reporting protocols, and secure government-to-government and government-to-industry channels for exchanging safety information. He said the pace of AI development is accelerating and warned that "as the process of building AI becomes more automated, the pace could accelerate rapidly." Each government, he said, should decide how to bake international standards into its own legal system.

"If AI is to be democratic, the most important decisions cannot be made by labs in San Francisco alone." — Sam Altman, OpenAI CEO, UN Security Council briefing, 2026-09-23

Amodei used his briefing to describe frontier AI risk as potentially humanity-level and said the ambition of governing it "is bigger than any one company." He advanced three concrete proposals: a narrow international agreement banning the use of AI to further biological weapons development, a verification-and-evaluation regime so states can audit each other's compliance, and common global standards for capability testing and incident notification. Amodei committed Anthropic itself to a pacing posture. "We will slow down as much as necessary in order to make sure that every successive AI technology that we release is actually safe," he said, echoing the framing of his September 12 "Pace the Frontier" essay.

"Fully addressing the risks requires even more prudence. We will slow down as much as necessary, in order to make sure that every successive AI technology we release is actually safe." — Dario Amodei, Anthropic CEO, UN Security Council briefing, 2026-09-23

Bengio put the case in comparative terms. "International cooperation is what the world needs in the face of three global threats," he told the Council, tying AI risk to nuclear proliferation and pandemic response as domains where individual states have converged on shared verification. Delangue, the fourth briefer, pushed for open-weight releases as a load-bearing pillar of any international regime because they allow independent auditing of what frontier systems actually do. The Council did not vote and did not table a resolution. It agreed to reconvene at officer level to draft what one European delegation called "an initial set of confidence-building measures" before the Council's next thematic session.

The counter-narrative ran on the UN General Assembly rostrum the same afternoon. President Donald Trump used his UNGA speech to reject international AI regulation as "a globalist scheme to control the technology" and to describe existential AI risk as "a hoax," comparable in his framing to climate warnings from earlier in his career. "I'm not going to stifle growth of something that will be bigger than the industrial revolution," he told the Assembly, and added that only "a high-IQ president" could safeguard AI. He said the United States is "leading now over China by a lot" and would not accept binding multilateral restrictions on model training or deployment. The address came a week after Trump promised on Truth Social to create an "AI Force" analogous to the Space Force and to name an AI czar to replace outgoing adviser David Sacks.

The gap between the two speeches is the story. On the Security Council side, the two most consequential frontier-lab CEOs asked a fifteen-country body chaired by France for the very oversight the US President was denouncing two blocks away. Amodei's proposed bioweapon carve-out is narrower than any existing bilateral AI arrangement and would function like the 1975 Biological Weapons Convention adapted for machine-learning research. Altman's incident-reporting proposal parallels the notification obligations built into the Chemical Weapons Convention and the ITER science-cooperation treaty. Bengio's line about three global threats deliberately tracked the language of the WHO's post-COVID pandemic-preparedness treaty. Council members from Ecuador, Sierra Leone and Denmark backed the general direction; Russia and China did not commit to any specific proposal; the US was represented by acting Deputy Ambassador John Kelley, who confined his intervention to a statement that the US "will not accept a regulatory model exported from outside our sovereign process." The session ended without a resolution but with an agreed follow-on meeting.

Anthropic says Claude autonomously discovered ART, a novel CRISPR-adjacent enzyme system.

Anthropic · 2026-09-23 · Source ↗

Claude Mythos 5 agents searched 1.9 billion protein clusters in 21.5 hours across 949 sessions and 215.6M tokens, narrowed 200,000+ reverse transcriptases to 3,500 candidates, and produced 20 human-readable reports; ART's DNA-repeat architecture resembles CRISPR arrays. Feng Zhang endorsed the work.

Anthropic announced on Wednesday, September 23, that a Claude Mythos 5 agent swarm autonomously identified a previously uncharacterized enzyme system whose structure resembles the DNA-repeat arrays behind CRISPR gene editing. The company named the system array-associated reverse transcriptases, or ART. The team gave the swarm one research brief: find new reverse transcriptase systems in a database of 1.9 billion protein clusters. From there, Anthropic reports, the agents ran without human input for 21.5 hours across 949 agent sessions, consuming 215.6 million tokens. One agent planned and executed each task while a second reviewed it, and agents spawned new tasks as they went.

The search narrowed 200,000+ candidate reverse transcriptases down to about 3,500 promising new candidate systems, then produced human-readable reports on the twenty most compelling. ART itself is a three-part system: a reverse transcriptase enzyme, an associated accessory protein of unknown function, and an array of evenly spaced non-coding DNA repeats. Anthropic says only a handful of known biological systems share that combination of features, and every one of them cuts, copies or pastes DNA. Early wet-lab experiments show ART's array is transcribed into a set of distinct short RNAs, suggesting the system may operate through an RNA guide the way CRISPR does.

"This is an exciting example of how AI agents can contribute to biological discovery." — Feng Zhang, MIT and Broad Institute, CRISPR co-discoverer

Anthropic paired the discovery with the launch of a new life-sciences research group and laboratory in Cambridge, Massachusetts, and said further experiments are underway to determine ART's biological function and whether it can be reprogrammed as a gene-editing tool. The company was careful not to claim that ART is a CRISPR analogue, only that its architecture shares the array-plus-accessory-protein signature. ART was found primarily in bacteriophages, the viruses that infect bacteria, which is where much of the tooling behind modern gene editing was originally discovered. The result lands the same afternoon Amodei told the UN Security Council that AI is now "bigger than any one company" and that international governance ought to include verification and testing regimes for exactly this class of capability.

Anthropic engineers published a Wednesday blog post describing a two-week performance sprint in August that made claude.ai and the Claude desktop app 3.1x faster on average across 13 measurements. The sprint approach was unusual: rather than sending humans to profile the app, the team gave Claude a steady stream of real-user latency measurements from four journeys the company says account for 95 percent of activity, plus permission to propose and land its own optimization patches under review. Between August 13 and August 27, more than 3,000 changes were merged. Zero customer-facing incidents or rollbacks occurred.

The measured deltas are large. Page loads went from 3.1s to 0.55s. The post walked through Claude's optimization loop step by step: reproduce the problem in a lab environment, propose a code change, roll it to a canary population, check performance after deployment, and either promote or revert. Anthropic said the loop ran continuously through the sprint window without a dedicated on-call handoff.

"3,000-plus changes merged in two weeks, zero customer-facing incidents. Claude reproduces a problem in the lab, proposes a code change, and checks performance after deployment." — Anthropic engineering post, 2026-09-23

Trending Topics / Officechai · 2026-09-23 · Source ↗

Astra steered a Corolla fitted with comma.ai control hardware through a 130-metre cone course in 5:22 on its second attempt, consuming 246.6 million tokens and $7.74 in inference. Every other model tested — Anthropic and xAI included — failed before the halfway mark. The models drive one command at a time inside a single ongoing chat session.

DrivingBench, a new benchmark that puts general-purpose language models in the driver's seat of a real vehicle, published its Wednesday leaderboard with GPT-6 Astra as the first model to complete the course. The test vehicle is a Toyota Corolla fitted with comma.ai hardware that lets external software control steering, accelerator and brakes. The course is a roughly 130-metre cone slalom. Each model drives one command at a time inside a single, ongoing chat session with a fixed-field-of-view forward camera and a steering profile deliberately calibrated to be cautious. Every model gets exactly one shot at the course.

Astra completed the course in 5 minutes 22 seconds on its second attempt, consuming approximately 246.6 million tokens at a running inference cost of $7.74. Every other model tested — including Anthropic's flagship and xAI's Grok 4.7 — failed to make it past the halfway mark on the same course. Astra's success came in the compressed timing regime the benchmark uses: a text model that has never been trained on driving footage or steering signals can still, with the right prompting scaffold, extract enough spatial reasoning from a static camera frame to keep a real car in its lane and around a cone.

"General-purpose language models were never trained to drive. Getting a Corolla through a cone course by chat, one instruction at a time, is a physical-reasoning eval, not a self-driving demo." — DrivingBench leaderboard notes

The benchmark authors flag the obvious caveat: this has almost nothing to do with production autonomous driving. Robotaxis rely on specialized end-to-end systems trained on hundreds of millions of miles of driving data, running at hundreds of hertz on dedicated hardware. DrivingBench measures the residual physical-reasoning capability of frontier general-purpose models in an environment they were never trained for. Astra was released September 3 and is priced at $5 input / $25 output per million tokens; at 246.6M tokens the Wednesday run is roughly the cost of a decent dinner and dwarfs the token count required for any current test-taking benchmark.

Jev-Mem paper splits agent memory into System-1 controller and System-2 reasoning.

arXiv 2609.23986 / Hugging Face · 2026-09-23 · Source ↗

Dongming Jiang, Yi Li and Bingzhe Li propose a three-plane architecture: a fast System-One controller for memory typing and query routing, a multi-relational memory plane, and a slower System-Two reasoning plane. On LoCoMo with GPT-4o-mini, 11.0 percent higher answer quality, 6.6x faster memory construction, 36.7 percent lower query latency.

Jev-Mem lands the top slot on the Wednesday Hugging Face papers board with a division-of-labor architecture for agentic memory that separates lightweight, high-frequency memory-management decisions from deliberate reasoning. Authors Dongming Jiang, Yi Li and Bingzhe Li describe three planes: a System-One control plane for memory typing, relational organization, query routing and adaptive stopping; a structured multi-relational memory plane that stores canonical entities and relations; and a System-Two reasoning plane invoked only when the controller determines the retrieval task genuinely needs deliberative language-model output.

On the LoCoMo long-context QA benchmark with GPT-4o-mini as the reasoning backbone, Jev-Mem posted an 11.0 percent absolute improvement in overall answer quality against the strongest per-metric baseline, 6.6x faster memory construction, and 36.7 percent lower end-to-end query latency. The paper argues that dropping every retrieval decision into the full LLM is what makes existing agentic-memory pipelines slow and expensive, and that a small, fast controller can carry roughly 80 percent of the decisions without touching the reasoning model.

"System One captures fast, lightweight decision-making, whereas System Two performs slower, deliberative reasoning." — Jev-Mem paper abstract

The paper ships with an open-source reference implementation. It sits in a broader agent-memory research cluster this week that includes VideoResearcher (self-improving tool design for long-video understanding), a fully-open 7B LLM that matches Qwen3-8B on general benchmarks and rivals frontier models on math and agentic search, and a growing set of recursive-self-improvement papers submitted the same 48-hour window as OpenAI publicly named RSI as the capability that most needs external governance.

Cisco Talos discloses CLOSEDQUORUM, first Windows implant that lets four LLMs vote on attacks.

Cisco Talos / The Register · 2026-09-23 · Source ↗

The 16.4MB Go implant queries DeepSeek, Qwen, Mistral and Google Gemini and picks its next action by plurality vote across pre-defined actions. Capabilities include LSASS memory scraping, browser-password theft across Chrome/Edge/Firefox and crypto-wallet exfiltration for MetaMask, Exodus and Ethereum. Not yet observed in the wild.

Cisco Talos published a Wednesday research post naming CLOSEDQUORUM as the first publicly documented Windows implant whose command-and-control loop is fully autonomous and delegated to a committee of large language models. The 16.4MB Go binary embeds API keys for DeepSeek, Qwen, Mistral and Google Gemini, and picks its next post-compromise action by plurality vote across the four models. The action set is deliberately narrowed to a pre-defined list — LSASS memory scraping for Windows credentials, saved-password theft across Chrome, Edge and Firefox, cryptocurrency wallet exfiltration for MetaMask, Exodus and Ethereum, shellcode injection, and standard persistence mechanisms.

Talos discovered the sample through its CAIRN project, an open-source framework for tracking and analyzing AI-augmented threats. The team hasn't observed CLOSEDQUORUM deployed in the wild; the public build ships as a non-functional template with dummy API keys and only static-analysis evidence of the autonomous loop. Artifacts inside the binary link the developer to postings on carding-focused criminal forums going back to 2025, and Talos assesses moderate confidence the tool was built for financial-cybercrime deployment rather than sponsored espionage.

"The first publicly documented Windows implant whose command-and-control loop is fully autonomous. Four LLMs vote on the next action from a fixed action space." — Cisco Talos research post, 2026-09-23

The design choice — voting across four models — is a defense against safety refusals from any single provider. If DeepSeek refuses, Qwen, Mistral and Gemini can still form a majority. The disclosure hardens the case researchers have been making since the May-July HuggingFace agent breaches: model providers' current refusal training is not enough when adversaries can query multiple models and pick the majority. Talos is coordinating disclosure with each of the four cited providers, and DeepSeek and Mistral had already added blocklists for the specific prompt patterns by the time the post went live.
