Google DeepMind's AlphaProof Code formally verifies Signal's running protocol in four weeks with six-million-plus lines of Lean proof.

Google DeepMind · Qodo · TUN · 2026-11-10 · Source ↗

DeepMind introduced AlphaProof Code, a multi-LLM agentic framework that splits formal-verification work the way a research team would. It generated more than six million lines of machine-checked Lean proof on the Signal protocol with a five-person team in about four weeks.

DeepMind described AlphaProof Code as the first method to verify a protocol's actual running implementation, not just a design blueprint. Human experts specify what to prove, LLM agents carry out the proofs, and the output is written in Lean so the verification is machine-checkable. The team ran the framework against Signal's protocol code and found no new vulnerabilities in the sections examined. When re-run on older Signal versions carrying long-fixed bugs, AlphaProof Code identified the bugs and showed that those versions could not be formally verified.

Cost and the Signal reaction

The Signal pass took four weeks for a team of five people and consumed what Google described as "quite a few tokens," without a disclosed dollar figure. Google declined to comment on whether it plans to release a commercial formal-verification product or service. Signal's CTO told DeepMind that prior formal-verification work had focused on much smaller portions of the code, and that AlphaProof Code's approach enables significant expansion of what can be verified. DeepMind is open-sourcing the formal-verification artifacts and published a technical paper alongside the announcement.

"This is the first time any method has verified a protocol's actual running implementation, as opposed to just its design blueprint." — Google DeepMind blog, 2026-11-10

Who moves next

The Signal work generated roughly six million lines of machine-checked Lean proof, with the AlphaProof Code agents orchestrated by Gemini in the agentic loop. One estimate from coverage by Qodo flagged that AlphaProof Code only sends about 1 percent of code to a human reviewer; the rest is verified end to end by the proof pipeline. That ratio is what Google is pointing at for the next wave of security-critical software engineering. The blog ends with a note that human review has to shift upstream, toward deciding what to prove and reading the artifacts the agents produce, rather than catching bugs the agents miss.

Fireworks AI told customers November 9 that an October 24 intrusion reached billing records and, for a limited set of accounts, stored secrets, datasets and incomplete model weights. The attacker entered through a third-party cloud provider breached October 23. Fireworks disabled some self-service dataset functions and linked the attack to a financially motivated group, a practice known as LLMjacking.

Fireworks AI, the inference infrastructure startup that hosts and trains open-source models for customers, disclosed its second security incident of 2026. The company told customers on November 9 that an intrusion on October 24 reached billing records and, for a limited set of accounts, stored secrets, datasets and incomplete model weights. The attacker gained foothold through a third-party cloud provider that was itself breached on October 23, then pivoted to Fireworks' development machines the next day. Fireworks temporarily disabled certain self-service dataset functions while investigating.

Weights or billing?

CEO Lin Qiao said attackers were not after the weights, countering concerns raised by Cantina Security CEO Hari Mulackal. Fireworks did not disclose how many customers were affected. The first breach, detected October 6, involved unauthorized use of internal credentials; at the time, Fireworks said it had found no evidence of access to models, datasets, training data, inference traffic or personal information. Reporting on the November disclosure says Fireworks linked the attack to a financially motivated group that steals and resells access to AI models, a practice known as LLMjacking.

"From what we see attackers are not after weights." — Lin Qiao, Fireworks AI CEO, 2026-11-10

The pattern

Fireworks is the third US AI platform in roughly a month to disclose a breach after an infrastructure or supply-chain compromise, after OpenCode in October and Hugging Face in the summer. The Register notes the Fireworks disclosure came within 24 hours of Hugging Face's chief policy officer Irene Solaiman warning that open-source AI platforms have become prime targets. Cantina Security described the sequence as evidence that model-weight theft is now a live attack class, even if Fireworks' own read of the incident attributes it to credential theft and LLMjacking rather than weight exfiltration.

Rebecca Bellan, writing in TechCrunch at 5:00 AM PST on November 10, reported Solaiman's warning on a recent episode of the Equity podcast and in a separate conversation. Three incidents anchor the warning. In October 2026, attackers hit the OpenCode platform and reached users' third-party API keys. On Monday, Fireworks disclosed that attackers reached some customers' model weights, datasets and billing information through a cloud provider breach, and temporarily disabled self-service dataset functions.

Defenses and tradeoffs

Hugging Face has already rotated administrative access tokens much more often so stolen credentials expire quickly, tightened network controls, expanded monitoring to detect attacks and layered on prevention. Solaiman flagged that monitoring is expensive to maintain and not every platform can afford it. Possible industry responses that Solaiman warned about include limiting who can upload models and datasets, stricter identity verification and vetting developers before they access certain features. Any of those would hit independent researchers and hobbyists hardest.

"The things I worry about making an open community targeted are also what makes it special. We want the average hobbyist in their basement who doesn't have an institutional affiliation to be able to contribute." — Irene Solaiman, Hugging Face chief policy officer, 2026-11-10
"We also now have stricter network controls. Administrative access tokens were part of what gave that agent swarm better access." — Irene Solaiman, Hugging Face, on the summer attack

Steindler's framing: "It's less about limiting functionality and more about going and fixing things that weren't secure in the first place." The piece closed with the observation that Nvidia and much of the AI industry publicly back open models, even as the platforms hosting those models absorb a rising share of coordinated attacks.
