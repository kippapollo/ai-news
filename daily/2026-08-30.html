DeepSeek closes on $7.4 billion at a $74 billion pre-money mark, sets 2027 Shanghai IPO

The cap table

Existing shareholders Monolith Management, Shixiang Capital and battery giant Contemporary Amperex Technology Limited (CATL) are re-upping their positions. Where the money goes

Proceeds are targeted at model R&D and compute infrastructure. The company had held off outside financing through that episode. A $7.4 billion raise at $74 billion pre-money represents an implicit revenue multiple of roughly eight times reported annualised revenue in the mid-single-digit billions, and puts DeepSeek at approximately 4 percent of Anthropic's May 2026 Series H post-money mark of $965 billion and roughly one-quarter of the $300 billion valuation OpenAI is reportedly seeking in tender-offer talks.

The IPO track

DeepSeek intends to file its IPO prospectus with the China Securities Regulatory Commission by the end of 2026, targeting a first-half 2027 debut on the Shanghai Stock Exchange's Science and Technology Innovation Board (Star Market). The listing would be the largest domestic Chinese AI IPO to date and functions as a policy signal: Beijing has pushed since 2023 to build an AI-financing rail that does not depend on Nasdaq or the New York Stock Exchange, and the DeepSeek listing would be the flagship test of that pipeline. Bloomberg on Saturday framed Chinese sovereign and quasi-sovereign capital as now the single largest funding pool for domestic AI compute buildouts.

OpenAI cuts off Cursor after SpaceX acquisition, sets November 12 termination date

The Decoder · 2026-08-29 · Source ↗

OpenAI notified Cursor on Saturday that it will terminate the coding-agent's API contract on November 12, 2026, the first material supplier fallout from SpaceX's early-August acquisition of Anysphere. OpenAI Head of Developer Experience Thibault Sottiaux told The Decoder the decision "boils down to trust" and cited Elon Musk's history of breaking Twitter/X data terms and unauthorised use of competitor model outputs to train Grok; Cursor CEO Michael Truell said OpenAI accounts for only about five percent of Cursor's AI traffic and Anthropic Post-Training Lead Tom Brown publicly committed to expand compute for Cursor.

The Cursor cutoff is the first concrete downstream effect of SpaceX's early-August acquisition of Anysphere, the company behind the Cursor coding agent. OpenAI's rationale, delivered by Head of Developer Experience Thibault Sottiaux to The Decoder, foregrounds precedent rather than any specific incident on the Cursor side: OpenAI "can't be confident that SpaceX will follow the terms of service," Sottiaux said, pointing to Elon Musk's history of breaking Twitter/X data-access contracts and to the unauthorised use of competitor model outputs to train xAI's own Grok family.

"It boils down to trust." — Thibault Sottiaux, OpenAI Head of Developer Experience

Cursor's response

Cursor CEO Michael Truell downplayed the operational impact, telling The Decoder OpenAI models make up "only about five percent of Cursor's AI traffic" and noting that "Cursor was one of the very first users of OpenAI." The five-percent figure aligns with public benchmarks that show Cursor users default overwhelmingly to Anthropic's Claude Sonnet and Opus for coding work, with GPT models used only in specific niches. Anthropic Post-Training Lead Tom Brown responded publicly that Cursor has been "a trusted partner since Sonnet 3.5" and that Anthropic will "continue to expand compute capacity" for the coding agent, positioning the lab as the primary beneficiary of the cutoff.

The escalating pattern

Saturday's cutoff continues a chain of frontier labs refusing to serve competitors that has run through August. OpenAI cut Anthropic's Claude Code from the OpenAI API earlier in the month. Anthropic revoked OpenAI's Claude API access to prevent evaluation runs. Cursor's SpaceX ownership now places it in the same "adversarial customer" category, alongside xAI itself. The pattern makes multi-provider coding agents structurally harder to build: an agent that depended on being able to swap between GPT and Claude for load balancing or capability specialisation now needs a fallback plan that assumes one or both APIs may be revoked on short notice for competitive reasons.

What Musk gets

SpaceX's Cursor acquisition, disclosed earlier in August, gives Elon Musk a coding-agent front end to distribute Grok and any future xAI reasoning models against the frontier labs' offerings. The November 12 cutoff date pushes OpenAI's exit past Grok 5's expected fall launch window, meaning Cursor customers will have roughly ten weeks to migrate any GPT-dependent workflows before the API stops responding. No statements were sourced from SpaceX or Musk directly in the article.

Sony Music Publishing, Warner Chappell and additional publishers filed suit against Anthropic and co-founders Dario Amodei and Benjamin Mann in the Northern District of California, alleging a "brazen campaign of illegally torrenting, scraping, and downloading" thousands of copyrighted musical works to train Claude. The case is the third major music-industry action against the lab after August 2025's $1.5 billion Bartz settlement and Concord/Universal's January 2026 complaint, and lands weeks before Anthropic's rumored fall IPO at a valuation reported as high as $2 trillion.

TechCrunch's Kirsten Korosec reported Saturday that Sony Music Publishing, Warner Chappell Music, and a coalition of additional music publishers filed a fresh copyright infringement suit against Anthropic and co-founders Dario Amodei and Benjamin Mann in the US District Court for the Northern District of California late Friday. The complaint characterises Claude's music-related training-data acquisition as "blatant theft" and "flagrant piracy," alleging Anthropic used torrenting, unauthorised scraping and pirate downloads to obtain thousands of copyrighted musical compositions and lyric transcriptions.

"Brazen campaign of illegally torrenting, scraping, and downloading copyrighted works." — Complaint, Sony Music Publishing et al.

The Bartz template

The publishers explicitly track the theory of liability that produced Anthropic's $1.5 billion settlement in the Bartz v. Anthropic authors' class action in August 2025. Judge William Alsup's Bartz ruling held that training on copyrighted material can qualify as fair use, but that acquiring the underlying works through piracy does not. The music-publisher plaintiffs pick up that second half of the ruling, removing the fair-use defense that would otherwise have been Anthropic's strongest counter and giving the case a well-tested procedural path to summary judgment.

Named co-founders

The complaint names Dario Amodei and Benjamin Mann as personal defendants, a departure from earlier AI IP litigation that typically named only the corporate entity. The individual naming signals the publishers intend to pierce the corporate veil and pursue personal liability for the decisions to acquire training data through unauthorised channels.

"We disagree with the publishers' claims and we intend to defend ourselves robustly in court." — Anthropic spokesperson

The IPO overhang

The suit's timing matters because Anthropic's annualised revenue crossed $65 billion in July per Bloomberg, and the company is preparing for a fall or year-end IPO at a valuation reported by Bloomberg as high as $2 trillion. Underwriters will need any material litigation reserve requirement or contingent-liability disclosure resolved before an S-1 filing. The complaint does not disclose a total damages number but relies on the federal statutory damages framework of up to $150,000 per willfully infringed work; industry lawyers cited by TechCrunch said the plaintiff coalition could plausibly claim damages in the low-single-digit billions on the underlying catalogue. That range would be a material S-1 risk-factor disclosure and could compress IPO price talk. The filing is Anthropic's second major legal event in the same week, following US District Judge Rita Lin's ruling that the Pentagon acted unlawfully in blacklisting the company.

Google WikiSkill lifts Gemini-3.5-Flash agent accuracy 18.6 points via a self-edited wiki

The Decoder · arXiv 2608.27454 · 2026-08-29 · Source ↗

Google Research's WikiSkill sits on three layers — an immutable Raw layer of execution traces, an editable Wiki layer of distilled lessons and a Skill layer of active procedural instructions — with a Wiki Maintainer, Skill Proposer and gating validator moving data between them. Across five benchmarks the framework lifted Gemini-3.5-Flash from 49.5 to 68.1 percent (+18.6) and Qwen-3.6-27B from 39.4 to 63.3 percent (+23.9), with LiveMath on Gemini jumping from 33.0 to 72.6 percent.

WikiSkill (arXiv 2608.27454, authors Liyan Tang, Cyrus Rashtchian, Chun-Sung Ferng, Andrew Tomkins, Da-Cheng Juan and Tu Vu at Google Research) targets what the authors call the "amnesia problem" in prior agent skill-evolution approaches: earlier systems generated useful insights during each task and then discarded them at the end of the cycle. WikiSkill instead makes agent experience persistent and editable, allowing the same skill library to grow across thousands of task runs.

The three layers

The framework organises agent memory into three layers. A Raw Layer stores complete, immutable execution traces of every task attempt. A Wiki Layer holds distilled structured insights about failures and successes, updated continuously as new traces arrive. A Skill Layer contains the active procedural instructions the agent consults at inference time, with rollback if performance regresses.

The four components

Four components move data through the layers. The inference agent consults the current skill library on each task. A Wiki Maintainer consolidates new traces into structured lessons in the wiki layer. A Skill Proposer drafts candidate procedural updates from wiki entries. A gating validator checks proposals against held-out tasks before promoting them into the active skill layer.

"The agent, aka the model, doesn't truly learn in a continuous sense. But it writes better instructions for itself after each run." — Matthias Bastian, The Decoder

The results

Across five benchmarks spanning LiveMath, SpreadSheet, web search, document QA and interactive virtual-environment tasks, Gemini-3.5-Flash rose from 49.5 to 68.1 percent average performance and Qwen-3.6-27B from 39.4 to 63.3 percent. The biggest single-benchmark deltas were LiveMath on Gemini (33.0 to 72.6 percent) and SpreadSheet on Gemini (50.5 to 76.6 percent). Ablations confirmed the accumulated wiki entries are the load-bearing component. The authors argue skill evolution "complements model scaling," with smaller models occasionally outperforming substantially larger ones once the wiki has matured. The underlying model weights are unchanged; only the instructions the agent writes for itself get better.

The reasoning interface

LAION releases 10 million hours of open video data as the Big Video Dataset

The Decoder · 2026-08-29 · Source ↗

The LAION collective published the Big Video Dataset (BVD), covering 55 million clips derived from 80 million videos and 1.3 billion source URLs, with auto-generated video and audio descriptions plus 300 million still frames extracted from the same source. Models trained on BVD beat comparable InternVid-trained models by up to 2.1 percentage points on video-to-text benchmarks, positioning the release as the open counterpart to Google's WebVid and VideoCC.

The LAION collective published the Big Video Dataset (BVD) on August 29, releasing what is now the largest open-license video corpus targeted at video-language pretraining. The pipeline started from 1.3 billion CommonCrawl video URLs, downloaded 80 million videos (predominantly English, primarily YouTube-sourced), and extracted 55 million shorter clips totaling ten million hours of footage. Each clip ships with auto-generated video descriptions and audio descriptions to support text-video contrastive training and video captioning objectives, and the release adds 300 million still images pulled from the same source frames.

"Models trained on BVD outperform comparable models trained on InternVid by up to 2.1 percentage points on common video-to-text benchmarks." — LAION release notes

The scale gap it closes

Academic and open-source video foundation model efforts have until now had to reach for InternVid, WebVid-10M or scraped-YouTube subsets that were one order of magnitude smaller than what Google's WebVid/VideoCC pipelines and Meta's internal HowTo100M-scale corpora provided. BVD raises the open-source ceiling to roughly the same scale as the closed-shop text-video pretraining runs at frontier labs. LAION released the dataset and companion code under a research-only license and asked users to respect original creators' rights.

The commercial context

The release lands as demand for open video data intensifies alongside launches like Google's Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash the same day, and as commercial video models — ByteDance Seedance, Alibaba Wan 3.0, Google Veo 3.1 — push toward per-second-of-1080p pricing floors near ten cents. Open weights trained on BVD would offer a research-priced alternative to the API-metered commercial offerings and give academic labs the ability to reproduce the training-data conditions inside a public corpus.

TechCrunch reads Nvidia's post-Blackwell moat as rack-scale orchestration, not raw GPU throughput

TechCrunch · 2026-08-29 · Source ↗

TechCrunch AI editor Russell Brandom argued Saturday that Nvidia's competitive advantage has migrated from GPU FLOPs to whole-rack orchestration as inference deployments cross the gigawatt line and data movement becomes the binding constraint. Nvidia VP of Storage Technology Jason Hardy told Brandom the Vera Arm CPU delivers "upwards of 3x improvement" on data-orchestration-limited operations by letting a Rubin rack lean on flash-storage tiers without stalling GPUs on I/O.

TechCrunch's AI editor Russell Brandom published a strategic analysis on Saturday, threading Nvidia's Q2 FY27 print of $96.2 billion, the AWS 2-million-GPU expansion and the OpenAI Jalapeño inference-chip paper into a single argument: raw GPU throughput no longer settles the buying decision at gigawatt scale. As inference scales across hundreds of racks, the bottleneck migrates from FLOPs to data movement, and Nvidia has moved earliest into that layer with Vera Rubin.

The Vera Rubin design

The Vera Rubin architecture pairs an Arm-based Vera CPU with Rubin GPUs via NVLink Fusion, adds a Groq 3 LPX inference accelerator alongside storage and networking silicon, and ships as a full-stack rack rather than a GPU-plus-NVLink bundle. Nvidia storage VP Jason Hardy provided the load-bearing quotes: "Vera is important because there's only so much memory that you can put in a single server or any sort of compute platform," Hardy said, describing how the Vera CPU lets a Rubin rack lean on flash-storage tiers without stalling GPUs waiting on I/O.

"We saw upwards of 3x improvement in these operations, where the Vera CPU is allowing for acceleration." — Jason Hardy, Nvidia VP of Storage Technology

The competitive read

The competitive implication drawn by Brandom is that AMD's Instinct MI400 series, Meta's MTIA 400 disclosed at Hot Chips 2026, and OpenAI-Broadcom's Jalapeño may match or exceed Nvidia on peak FLOPs per watt while still losing at rack- or campus-level total cost of ownership, because Nvidia now owns the orchestration primitives. Brandom cites OpenAI's own framing that Jalapeño was engineered "to minimize data movement and communication delays" — a tacit acknowledgment that even OpenAI's custom silicon prioritises orchestration over raw density.

What sits underneath

Vera Rubin systems are ramping into full production at CoreWeave, Google Cloud, Azure, Oracle and Nebius per Jensen Huang's Q2 remarks. The BlueField-4 DPU unveiled on Tuesday functions as the invisible plumbing that lets Vera Rubin scale to 72-node NVL domains at 1,200 W per socket. Brandom's essay does not include new revenue guidance or unit-shipment claims; it stands as an analytical piece rather than a reportorial one, and drew immediate uptake in investor Twitter given the same-day discussion of Nvidia's compressed 5.4 percent implied post-earnings move.

Fortune frames AI hyperscaler debt as $1.65 trillion "reverse crowding" of Treasury

Fortune via Yahoo Finance · 2026-08-29 · Source ↗

Fortune's Jason Ma argued Saturday that AI hyperscaler bond issuance has grown large enough to reverse the traditional causal arrow between Washington's fiscal footprint and corporate bond markets. Year-to-date investment-grade issuance through July hit $1.7 trillion (27 percent above the year-earlier pace), PIMCO and JPMorgan credit analysts identified roughly $1.65 trillion of hidden hyperscaler borrowing via SPVs, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent used a Bloomberg interview to acknowledge AI-issuer demand for capital is now large enough to affect Treasury auctions.

Fortune published a Saturday analysis reframing the traditional "crowding out" debate for the AI era, arguing that hyperscaler capex-linked borrowing has grown large enough to reverse the usual causal arrow between Washington's fiscal footprint and corporate bond markets. Three data points anchor the piece: $1.7 trillion of investment-grade corporate issuance through July of 2026 (27 percent above the year-earlier pace, on pace to top $2 trillion for the first time in history); roughly $1.65 trillion of hidden hyperscaler borrowing identified by PIMCO and JPMorgan credit analysts through wholly owned SPVs, joint-venture leases with private-credit funders like Blue Owl and Apollo, and equipment-financing conduits that do not roll up to parent balance sheets; and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's Bloomberg-interview acknowledgment that AI-issuer demand for capital is now large enough to affect Treasury auctions.

"We are also seeing big corporate issuance. The companies believe they're going to be so high. They don't really care what they're paying." — Scott Bessent, Treasury Secretary

The reverse-crowding thesis

The piece anchors to a $40 trillion total US federal debt stock, a projected fiscal 2026 deficit of roughly $2 trillion, and annual debt-servicing costs already at $1 trillion, arguing that AI capex is competing with the federal government for the same marginal dollar of long-duration credit. Ed Yardeni, quoted in the piece, calls it a mechanical funding-market phenomenon rather than a monetary-policy story: "Capital flowing into corporate bonds is capital not flowing into Treasuries, and Treasury yields have had to rise to clear the market." Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks are cited to note that "ever-expanding pools of capital are pouring into AI-related infrastructure."

The near-term forecast
