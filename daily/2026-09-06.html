Anthropic pays $1.5 billion to authors — largest copyright recovery in US history

Anthropic filed a proposed class-action settlement Friday September 5 in the Northern District of California in Bartz v. Anthropic, agreeing to pay at least $1.5 billion — roughly $3,000 per book across an estimated 500,000 works — for downloading pirated titles from LibGen and PiLiMi (Pirate Library Mirror) to train Claude. Judge William Alsup set preliminary approval for September 8, and Anthropic will destroy the pirated datasets across four installment payments through September 2027. The payout functions as a per-book price floor for every remaining AI training-data lawsuit and lands hours before Grady Hendrix and Jennifer Roberson filed the 49th US AI copyright case against Apple over Books3 training of OpenELM, represented by the same Susman Godfrey firm.

Policy · Safety · Bloomberg / NPR / Authors Guild · 2026-09-05 · Source ↗

Anthropic on Friday September 5 filed a proposed class-action settlement in the Northern District of California in Bartz v. Anthropic committing to pay at least $1.5 billion to resolve author claims that it downloaded pirated books from Library Genesis and Pirate Library Mirror to train Claude. The payment works out to roughly $3,000 per book across an estimated 500,000 titles, and it is the largest publicly reported copyright recovery in United States history. Class representatives are thriller novelist Andrea Bartz and nonfiction writers Charles Graeber and Kirk Wallace Johnson. Judge William Alsup set the preliminary-approval hearing for September 8 in the same courthouse where he had ruled in June that training Claude on lawfully purchased books can qualify as fair use but that sourcing the training corpus from shadow libraries cannot.

The settlement is structured as four installments over two years: October 2, 2025; April 30, 2026; September 25, 2026; and September 25, 2027. Anthropic will also destroy the pirated datasets. The payout is Anthropic's alone; the fair-use pathway that Judge Alsup blessed in June continues to protect Claude's training paradigm on legally acquired books going forward. The company's counsel described the payment as covering "legacy claims" that predate its shift to licensed and purchased data.

What the number does to the rest of the industry

The settlement lands as a per-book price floor for every remaining author copyright case pending against a frontier lab. OpenAI, Meta, Microsoft, Cohere, Perplexity, Nvidia, Databricks, Suno, Udio, Stability and, as of Friday afternoon, Apple are named defendants in some form of AI copyright complaint filed since the New York Times v. OpenAI action of December 2023. The Susman Godfrey firm that led the Anthropic case also filed the Apple complaint on behalf of horror novelist Grady Hendrix and fantasy author Jennifer Roberson in the same Northern District of California hours after the Anthropic term sheet reached the docket. Both cases target the Books3 pirated corpus and both seek statutory damages, disgorgement and destruction of the infringing models — the template Judge Alsup approved in the Anthropic settlement.

The Hendrix and Roberson complaint against Apple is the 49th US copyright lawsuit against an AI company and the 18th brought by book authors. Justin Nelson, the Susman Godfrey partner who led the Anthropic negotiation, told reporters after filing that "each of these cases raises the same fundamental question of who gets paid when a model learns from a book." The commercial read is that the class-action bar has now systematically mapped every major frontier lab against the same LibGen/PiLiMi source-of-truth and priced each infringing corpus at Alsup's $3,000-per-work benchmark.

"AI companies cannot simply steal authors' creative work to build their AI products and profit off of them." — Mary Rasenberger, Authors Guild CEO

What the number does to Anthropic

The $1.5 billion payout is roughly 3.5 percent of the $45 billion implied annual revenue run rate that Anthropic disclosed to Series F investors last week during its $183 billion valuation announcement. It is comfortably within the war chest but the timing matters: the settlement filed the same week Anthropic told investors it is on a fall IPO track sets a public expectation that similar payouts to other rights-holders will be part of the business through 2027. Anthropic paired the filing with a public commitment that its current training data pipeline sources from licensed publishers, purchased physical books scanned in-house, and synthetic data only.

"We remain committed to building safe AI systems that help people and organizations extend their capabilities, advance scientific discovery, and solve complex problems." — Aparna Sridhar, Anthropic Deputy General Counsel

The larger frame

Judge Alsup's June ruling had already split the frontier training-data question into two rulings the industry accepted: buy-and-scan is fair use; shadow-library scraping is not. Friday's settlement puts a dollar figure on the second half of that ruling. The larger implication for the AI industry is that pirated-corpus liability is now finite and priced. Every remaining defendant can now model exposure on its own case as "approximately $3,000 per book identified in a shadow library it can be shown to have pulled from," and the ambiguity that had made the class-action risk uncappable for eighteen months has resolved to a number.

Authors Guild CEO Mary Rasenberger, whose organization has led the plaintiff-coordinating effort across the 49 pending cases, called it "meaningful compensation" and framed it as the moment where AI companies "cannot simply steal creative work." Anthropic board chair Bret Taylor, who is separately being pressured by California Attorney General Rob Bonta and Delaware AG Kathleen Jennings on OpenAI child-safety issues the same day, did not comment on the settlement. The preliminary approval hearing on September 8 is expected to be routine.

"This landmark settlement will provide meaningful compensation for each class work and sets a precedent of AI companies paying for their use of pirated books." — Justin Nelson, Susman Godfrey partner and lead class counsel

Claude produces first end-to-end Lean proof of Fermat's Last Theorem across 11 days

Anthropic Research · 2026-09-04 · Source ↗

Anthropic says Claude produced a 13-million-line Lean proof of Fermat's Last Theorem including at least 29,500 intermediate theorems across 11 continuous days on the Prove2Me multi-agent platform. Andrew Wiles's original 1995 proof was 129 pages of natural-language mathematics; formalizing it in a proof assistant has been a running open target.

Anthropic published a research note Thursday September 4 claiming Claude, operating largely autonomously over 11 days on the Prove2Me platform, produced the first end-to-end computer-checked proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in the Lean proof assistant. The artifact runs to roughly 13 million lines of Lean and includes at least 29,500 intermediate theorems. Anthropic calls it the largest Lean proof ever assembled. Prove2Me, designed by Tianyi Peng and collaborators at Columbia University, maintains a directed acyclic graph of theorem statements and coordinates multiple Claude agents against it, allowing the system to schedule attempts, discharge lemmas, and verify progress mechanically before committing to the shared proof tree.

Andrew Wiles's original 1995 proof was 129 pages of natural-language mathematics and required months of human verification; formalizing it in an interactive theorem prover has been an open target in the mathematical community for a decade. Formal mathematicians reacted with a mix of enthusiasm and skepticism. Kevin Buzzard's Xena Project blog acknowledged Anthropic "beat me to it" on the formalization; others noted the Lean artifact still depends on a large set of imported lemmas from mathlib and questioned how much of the proof is Claude's novel reasoning versus assembly of prior work.

"In 11 days, working largely autonomously, Claude produced the first end-to-end, computer-checked proof of Fermat's Last Theorem. Along the way, it wrote 13 million lines of Lean and proved 29,500 intermediate theorems." — Anthropic Research

Regardless of that debate, the result is a landmark demonstration of long-horizon autonomous agent work — 11 continuous days of tool use, no human intervention on individual proof steps — and it hints at where frontier agent labs are steering: verifiable, formally checkable long-context work products that circumvent the "how do you evaluate agent outputs" problem entirely. Lean-checked artifacts either compile or they don't.

OpenAI paper argues benchmarks — not architecture — cause hallucinations

OpenAI Research · 2026-09-05 · Source ↗

Adam Tauman Kalai, Ofir Nachum, Santosh Vempala and Edwin Zhang argue that even under ideal pre-training on error-free data, models are pressured to hallucinate because generation is intrinsically harder than classification and post-training reinforces guessing because binary-graded benchmarks treat abstentions and wrong answers identically while assigning full credit to lucky guesses.

OpenAI published "Why Language Models Hallucinate" Friday September 5 by Adam Tauman Kalai, Ofir Nachum, Santosh Vempala (Georgia Tech) and Edwin Zhang, arguing hallucinations persist not because of a fundamental limit of language models but because standard training and evaluation procedures actively reward confident guessing over calibrated abstention. The paper proves that even under ideal pre-training on error-free data, models are pressured to hallucinate because generation is intrinsically harder than classification — the generative error rate is at least twice the classification (Is-It-Valid) error rate.

Post-training reinforces guessing because the dominant binary-graded benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, GPQA, SWE-bench, HLE) treat abstentions and wrong answers identically while assigning full credit to lucky guesses. The proposed fix: modify benchmark scoring rubrics to explicitly reward "I don't know" whenever a model's confidence sits below a stated threshold, mirroring negative marking used on the SAT and NMSQT. GPT-5 in reasoning mode is shown to hallucinate on 26 percent of open-ended questions in an internal SimpleQA-style eval while GPT-4o hallucinates on 52 percent.

"Hallucinations persist due to the way most evaluations are graded — language models are optimized to be good test-takers, and guessing when uncertain improves test performance." — OpenAI blog post

The paper drops as OpenAI's Preparedness Framework classifies advanced hallucination-triggered harms as a distinct risk vector, and it is co-timed with California SB 53 (frontier-AI transparency) advancing through Sacramento. Externally, the paper is read as OpenAI trying to move the evaluation industry — Artificial Analysis, LMSYS Arena, HELM — toward calibrated scoring rather than pure accuracy leaderboards.

Pennsylvania Act 35 of 2025 took effect Friday September 5, 60 days after Governor Josh Shapiro's July 7 signature, criminalizing the creation or distribution of "forged digital likenesses" — synthetic visual images or audio recordings of a real, identifiable person made without consent and passed off as genuine, with intent to defraud or injure. Sponsored by state Sen. Tracy Pennycuick (R-Montgomery), the statute amends the Pennsylvania forgery framework (18 Pa. C.S. § 4101) to add a new category covering both AI-generated video and voice cloning outside the robocall context.

Pennsylvania becomes one of a small handful of US states to criminalize AI voice cloning at that scope. Violations are graded as first-degree misdemeanors carrying $1,500-$10,000 fines and up to five years imprisonment, escalating to third-degree felonies (up to $15,000 fines and seven years) when done to defraud, coerce or commit theft. Washington state's Deceptive Synthetic Media Act (HB 1205) took effect the same July date on a parallel track.

"Deepfakes are a growing threat, and Pennsylvanians deserve strong protections against this kind of digital fraud." — Sen. Tracy Pennycuick (R-Montgomery), bill sponsor

Act 35 arrives against a widening federal patchwork: the TAKE IT DOWN Act became law in May, Tennessee's ELVIS Act protecting voice actors is a year old, and the No FAKES Act cleared Senate Judiciary in July. Pennsylvania's addition puts roughly two-thirds of US states under some form of criminal or civil deepfake statute as the 2026 election cycle approaches.

The CPython interpreter, the C-extension calling conventions, and the CUDA-specific glue code around PyTorch collectively make it impossible to write portable, high-performance ML code that runs across Nvidia, AMD, Apple Silicon and custom ASICs without rewriting hot paths in vendor-specific C++ or CUDA.

Mojo is his proposed answer: a Python superset with strong ownership, static compilation via MLIR, first-class GPU programming primitives, and a hardware-abstraction layer that targets multiple accelerator backends from one source. The HN discussion focused on whether the ecosystem inertia around PyTorch and CUDA is now too large to displace, and on whether Modular's commercial licensing terms will slow adoption relative to a fully open BSD or MIT release.

"Mojo is designed to combine the simplicity of Python with the performance of C and the safety of Rust. We need a language that lets you target any accelerator from a single source of truth." — Chris Lattner, CEO of Modular

The story is significant because Mojo has now moved from "interesting research project" to "actively pitched CUDA replacement," and Lattner is publicly framing hardware fragmentation — AMD MI300/MI400, Google TPU, AWS Trainium, Meta MTIA, Broadcom-custom XPUs — as the wedge that gives a new language a real shot.

Cloudflare's Kenton Varda reframes Durable Objects as "StatefulWorker"

Simon Willison / Cloudflare · 2026-09-05 · Source ↗

The Cloudflare Workers lead engineer proposes renaming Durable Objects to "StatefulWorker" to make the mental model obvious: a named Worker instance that holds persistent state (SQLite DB, container filesystem, in-memory state) that Cloudflare's runtime routes messages to by name across the global fleet. Durable Objects now underpin per-user agent sessions, per-conversation memory and per-tenant MCP sandboxes.

On Friday September 5, Simon Willison surfaced comments from Cloudflare's Kenton Varda — the lead engineer behind Workers and Durable Objects — arguing that Cloudflare's Durable Objects primitive is not conceptually complex, only badly named. Varda proposes renaming them "StatefulWorker" to make the mental model obvious: a Durable Object is a named Worker instance that holds persistent state (a SQLite database, container filesystem, or in-memory state), that Cloudflare's runtime routes messages to by name across the global fleet.

The reframing matters because Durable Objects are one of the most-used primitives in production agent infrastructure right now. They underpin per-user agent sessions, per-conversation memory, per-tenant sandboxes for MCP servers, and long-running WebSocket connections for real-time agent UIs. Several agent frameworks — including Cloudflare's own Agents SDK, and third-party stacks like Superduper — model an "agent session" as one Durable Object per user, giving each conversation its own consistent SQLite database and stateful actor.

"I think Durable Objects would be a lot less scary if we just called them StatefulWorker. A Durable Object is just a worker with persistent state." — Kenton Varda, Cloudflare

Varda's post reframes them as the AI-native cloud primitive for stateful, single-writer, globally-addressable agents — a positioning that puts Cloudflare's Workers platform directly opposite AWS Lambda plus DynamoDB and Vercel Fluid for the agent-runtime tier.
