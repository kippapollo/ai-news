The Financial Times' Sunday piece "Anthropic's best AI model struggles to attract users as cheaper tools thrive" put fresh numbers on a picture that Anthropic customers and OpenAI competitors have both been trying to read for weeks. "More performance is not worth the price tag." — Ara Kharazian, Ramp Economics Lab

The topline the mix numbers sit under

The FT overlays the adoption picture on run-rate revenue figures the paper attributes to people familiar with Anthropic's July run. What Fable is supposed to be, and what it is not

Anthropic priced Fable at $10 per million input tokens and up to $50 per million output at the largest context tier, positioning it as the top of the reasoning stack and the default the company wants Claude Code and the Claude.ai Team app to reach for on hard tasks. Enterprise buyers in Ramp's panel are already routing around the gap: they call Fable when the task visibly rewards deeper reasoning and default everything else to Opus 4.8, which is close enough on their canonical workloads that the marginal dollar goes further elsewhere. Anthropic's product routing changes — making Fable the Claude Code default, promoting it inside the Team app — have not overridden that choice on the API surface, which is what Ramp measures.

"Most people don't need to operate at the frontier." — Miles Clements, Partner, Accel

Drew Breunig calls Fable "the end of the free lunch," reframes coding-model choice as an allocation problem

dbreunig.com · Simon Willison · 2026-08-23 · Source ↗

Drew Breunig's Sunday essay argues that Claude Fable 5's pricing broke the "wait for the next model" default that had defined coding-agent economics for three years. Investment in coding harnesses, context strategies, and routing intelligence is now the load-bearing move, and Simon Willison boosted the piece into the Sunday must-read cluster within hours.

San Francisco technologist Drew Breunig published "Fable and The End of the Free Lunch" on his personal weblog on Sunday, August 23. The title deliberately borrows Herb Sutter's 2005 essay "The Free Lunch Is Over" — the piece that named the moment when the software industry had to stop counting on single-core clock scaling and learn parallelism because processors stopped getting linearly faster on their own. Breunig's analogy: the AI industry has just hit an equivalent break. Frontier labs will keep shipping smarter models, but they will not automatically be cheaper enough at each release cycle to make the routing problem go away.

"A new model would arrive at the same price (or cheaper!) and paper over most of your problems." — Drew Breunig, Fable and The End of the Free Lunch

The arithmetic underneath

Breunig walks through the numbers that broke the pattern. Claude Fable 5 lists at roughly $10 per million input tokens against Opus 4.8's roughly $5, GPT-5.6 Sol's $4 after OpenAI's August 21 cut, and open-weight competitors — GLM-5.2, Kimi K3, DeepSeek V4-Pro, Qwen3.8-Max — at prices that in some cases run under $1 per million. At those ratios, the marginal Fable call is only economically defensible for a small slice of tasks where the quality gap is load-bearing. Every other kind of coding work now belongs in a cheaper model with a better harness. The choice is no longer "which frontier model should I default to" but "how do I route this specific request to the model that best matches its difficulty and value."

Why harness investment is now rational

The essay's second half is the design argument. If a cheaper model is going to run the bulk of a session, the code around it — the harness, the context strategy, the tool selection, the prompt scaffolding, the sub-agent decomposition — has to compensate for whatever the cheaper model gives up on raw capability. Investments in prompt caching, context-window management, and skill packaging that seemed silly a year ago because the next model would handle it automatically are now essential. Breunig's proposed default: reserve Fable for design interrogation and complex reasoning, route routine coding tasks to cheaper alternatives, and invest engineering time in the routing layer itself.

"Prior to Fable, it felt silly to waste too much time improving your coding harness or context strategies." — Drew Breunig, Fable and The End of the Free Lunch

The "prompt debt" companion note

Breunig tied the essay to a companion note on X the same weekend about what he called "prompt debt" — the observation that Fable and Opus 5's occasional skill-breaking behaviors are a form of accumulated technical debt in prompts and skills that were engineered against older models with fundamentally different reasoning behaviors. Once a lab ships a new model that reasons differently, the skills built up against the old one need re-testing and often re-authoring. Skill versioning across models, in his frame, is now a real problem for any team shipping AI features at scale.

Access controls as a second forcing function

Beyond price, Fable's access controls and data-retention terms pushed some enterprises to reconsider not just token routing but token sourcing entirely — mixing self-hosted GLM or Kimi tokens with frontier tokens on a per-task basis rather than centralizing on a single vendor. Once a team has paid the fixed cost of building a routing layer, they tend not to tear it down even if a future Fable release drops the price. The moment ends up hardening a durable pattern of multi-vendor routing that was optional yesterday and standard tomorrow.

Who wins the shift

Breunig closes with what amounts to a research agenda for the next twelve months. Harness quality, prompt caching, and routing intelligence become more strategic than raw model choice. Labs that ship developer surface area — Anthropic Skills, OpenAI Responses caching, Google's context APIs — rather than pure model capability capture the incremental value the price ceiling now leaves on the table. Agent framework maintainers (Claude Agent SDK, Codex, Cursor, Aider, DeepSeek Harness) are the direct beneficiaries: the harness stopped being an implementation detail and became a purchasing criterion. Willison's boost of the essay closed the Sunday cluster and framed the two-post arc as the story of the day.

The converter is the load-bearing piece

The most impactful part of the PR is the converter work. jacekpoplawski's changes to convert_hf_to_gguf.py add three flags: --no-mtp to strip the NextN blocks, --mtp to emit MTP-only shards, and a default path that packs the base and MTP weights into a single combined GGUF for the loader to route at runtime. Users whose upstream GLM-4.5-Air weights on Hugging Face were exported without NextN blocks (an issue on some third-party quantizations) can now grab an MTP-only companion GGUF from jacekpoplawski's Hugging Face repository and side-load it via --model-draft, matching the pattern DeepSeek maintainers landed in April.

Vulkan-focused reviewer JohannesGaessler contributed independent testing showing Vulkan going as high as 1.25x on shorter prompts, closing much of the historical gap with CUDA for GLM-family speculative decoding. Reviewers confirmed no regressions on the two vision models most likely to break during graph refactoring — Solar Open and GLM-4.6V — before green-lighting the merge.

"1.19x mean speedup — baseline 74.55 tok/s → 88.73 tok/s with MTP." — PR #26534 benchmark table

llama.cpp rewrites vision-model resize for Pillow accuracy, discloses "most code written and verified by AI"

GitHub · ggml-org PR #27594 · 2026-08-23 · Source ↗

Xuan-Son Nguyen landed PR #27594 into b10598 Sunday at 17:16 UTC. The commit rebinds more than forty vision-language models — LLaVA-Next, Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL2/3, Gemma3-Vision, Pixtral, MiniCPM-V — to the resize algorithm their Hugging Face configs actually specify, ships an SSE/NEON bicubic path up to 53x faster on 336x336 to 3072x3072 upscales, and includes a rare on-the-record disclosure that most of the code was authored by an LLM.

Xuan-Son Nguyen, the llama.cpp multimodal lead who signs as ngxson, landed PR #27594 into b10598 on August 23 at 17:16 UTC. The changeset is mechanical but far-reaching. It removes every non-Pillow resize variant from the mtmd multimodal preprocessing pipeline and rebinds more than forty vision-language models to the resize algorithm their Hugging Face configs actually specify. The PR body opens with the plain-English rationale: Hugging Face transformers implementations universally rely on the Python Imaging Library (Pillow) for image resizing, and llama.cpp's parallel C++ implementations had accumulated a decade of subtle drift — different filter choices, different aliasing behavior, off-by-half-pixel bugs in the bilinear path — that showed up as sub-benchmark accuracy gaps on multimodal evaluations without any hint that the model was mis-fed.

"HuggingFace implementations exclusively use Pillow for image resizing." — PR #27594 rationale

The audit list

ngxson audited every mtmd-supported model — LLaVA, LLaVA-Next, MobileVLM, CogVLM2, MiniCPM-V, MiniCPM-o, InternVL2/2.5/3, Qwen2-VL, Qwen2.5-VL, Idefics3, Gemma3-Vision, Pixtral, Aria, Fuyu, and dozens more — and wrote per-model preprocessing configs that pick bilinear, bicubic, or lanczos exactly where the reference Python does. Bilinear got the biggest rewrite: it now produces PIL-exact output while retaining reasonable performance. Bicubic downsampling picked up an SSE/NEON-vectorized path that clocks up to 53x faster than the legacy scalar code on the 336x336 to 3072x3072 upscaling case common in high-resolution InternVL loads. Lanczos improved 1.4x to 3x depending on kernel size.

The disclosure buried in the PR description

The most consequential detail is buried in the PR body. ngxson labels the entire changeset a "mechanical task" and states outright that "most code written + verified by AI." That is a rare on-the-record acknowledgment from a maintainer at one of the most-scrutinized inference projects on GitHub that a merged multimodal-critical patch is largely LLM-authored. He documents his review workflow: an LLM writes the per-model config diff, an LLM writes the corresponding resize kernel or wrapper, ngxson runs the mtmd test suite plus targeted image-diff regression tests against Pillow reference outputs, then reviews any remaining hand-edited section. The PR passed CI on all backends and drew no blocking review comments before the merge.

"most code written + verified by AI" — ngxson, PR #27594 description

What this fixes downstream

The commit closes a class of reproducibility gaps that had been quietly biting the community for months. llama.cpp benchmark numbers for InternVL and Qwen-VL routinely trailed reported Python numbers by 1 to 4 percentage points on common VLM benchmarks, and the Pillow mismatch was one of the suspects. b10598 does not change model weights or grammar; it only ensures the pixels arriving at the vision encoder match what the model was trained on. For teams running mixed CUDA and Apple Silicon fleets to serve InternVL3 or Qwen2.5-VL to production traffic, the change means the observed accuracy gap between hosted transformers pipelines and self-hosted llama.cpp deployments should compress toward the noise floor.

"Up to 53x faster than legacy code (336x336 → 3072x3072 upscaling)." — PR #27594 benchmark table

The maintainer-workflow signal

The workflow ngxson describes — an LLM does the mechanical audit and writes the per-model configs, the human runs the test harness and reviews the diff — is the concrete version of the argument Breunig made in his same-day essay. Once the harness (in this case, the regression test suite) is good enough to catch what the LLM gets wrong, the human's job shifts from typing to reviewing. That the disclosure lands on a Sunday inside a project as widely deployed as llama.cpp is the practical proof point of what "AI-assisted maintainer work" looks like when the maintainer trusts the harness.

llama.cpp b10593 closes DeepSeek V4 multi-sequence rollback bug that could evict a neighbor's KV cache

GitHub · ggml-org PR #26756 · 2026-08-23 · Source ↗

Under the previous behavior, a rollback request from one sequence in a busy llama-server batch could partially clear the DeepSeek V4 attention cache for another sequence sharing the same graph — producing corrupt continuations with no error. The fix, co-authored with Georgi Gerganov, makes rollbacks single-use, scopes cache clears to specific sequence IDs, and validates compression ratios during graph load.

b10593 shipped at 11:40 UTC on Sunday, August 23, on commit b0539c4, through PR #26756. The build closes a rollback correctness bug in the DeepSeek V4 implementation that only surfaced under concurrent multi-sequence load — the exact configuration llama-server hosts hit when they serve multiple parallel DeepSeek V4 conversations to different users on the same GPU. Under the previous behavior, when llama-server accepted a rollback request from one sequence in a busy batch, the DeepSeek V4 attention cache could be partially cleared for another sequence sharing the same graph, producing corrupt continuations without any error or user-facing signal that the state had been evicted.

"resolving rollback functionality for multi-sequence scenarios" — b10593 changelog

What the fix actually does

The PR — co-authored with founder Georgi Gerganov — refactors the rollback path along four axes. First, each pending rollback becomes single-use, so it cannot silently reapply on a later batch. Second, cache clears scope to the specific sequence ID being rolled back rather than sweeping the entire graph. Third, the loader validates compression ratios during graph load so a malformed DeepSeek V4 checkpoint fails early rather than corrupting cache state at run time. Fourth, the graph topology is now static, so multi-sequence rollbacks cannot mutate shared state. Several TODO markers Gerganov had left during the initial DeepSeek V4 port are also cleared.

"making pending rollbacks single-use only" — PR #26756 description

The failure mode in production

Concretely, a llama-server host serving eight parallel DeepSeek V4 conversations on a single 8x H100 rig, where one user hits the regenerate button while a neighboring user is mid-generation, could see the neighbor's continuation degrade or start producing nonsense tokens for the rest of the session. Because the KV cache eviction was silent, operators had been chasing the bug as intermittent model-quality reports for weeks before the reproducer landed. The fix closes the specific race and makes the rollback path safe for shared-host deployment patterns that have become standard as DeepSeek V4 has grown into the fastest-growing self-hosted checkpoint in the community.

Two more Sunday cleanup commits

b10594 (13:21 UTC, PR #26692) is the tiniest of the day: the common device_info enumeration loop no longer runs when its output would not be printed, cutting several hundred milliseconds off cold start on multi-GPU boxes where the loop was reflecting on every visible device only to discard the result at higher log verbosities. b10595 (13:44 UTC, PR #27600) adds the LLAMA_SERVER_SLOTS_N_DIFF environment variable to llama-server. When set, the server emits a metric on every slot state change reporting the delta versus the previous poll — occupied slots gained, occupied slots lost, and a running high-water mark — so operators running llama.cpp behind a queue-based load balancer can feed slot-diff into their autoscaling loop without polling /metrics themselves.

Amanda Silberling's Sunday morning long-form for TechCrunch anchored the day's policy coverage with a plain-English audit of where US law actually stands on the training of large language models on copyrighted books. The through-line of the piece is that the question has been half-answered by a string of conflicting district-court rulings since the Anthropic settlement of 2025 and remains structurally unsettled because the Copyright Act itself has not been amended since 1976. Copyright doctrine hinges on the physical act of copying rather than on the downstream use of what is read, so an AI developer who ingests a million books is technically making a million reproductions even if the model that emerges does not itself contain any of those books.

"Copyright law hinges on copying, but it doesn't hinge on using the work or experiencing the work." — Cathy Gellis, IP/technology attorney

The folk analogy the piece walks through

Silberling interviewed Cathy Gellis, a longtime IP and technology attorney, who framed the analytic problem with a folk analogy. If you write a novel in Microsoft Word and run spell-check across it, nobody would suggest that Word owns the novel or that the spell-check pass grants Redmond a derivative interest. The piece uses that analogy to walk the reader up to the harder question: what happens when the model that studies the corpus is itself a commercial product designed to write the same kind of prose as the ingested authors.

"If you write your novel in Word and run spell check, we kind of feel comfortable with the idea of saying that Word does not own your novel." — Cathy Gellis

Alsup on transformative use

The piece points to Judge William Alsup's much-discussed 2025 order in the Anthropic litigation, in which Alsup wrote that "like any reader aspiring to be a writer, Anthropic's LLMs trained upon works not to race ahead and replicate or supplant them — but to turn a hard corner and create something different." That reasoning led Alsup to bless the training use even as he ordered a $1.5 billion settlement covering the pirated-source-acquisition portion of the case. The transformative-use frame is the ruling AI developers most want the circuit courts to inherit.

Bibas on where transformativeness fails

Silberling weighs Alsup's transformative-use logic against the Third Circuit's Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence outcome, where Judge Stephanos Bibas rejected the same fair-use theory on the ground that "Ross's use is not transformative because it does not have a 'further purpose or different character' than Thomson Reuters's." The distinction turned on Ross Intelligence's straight-line intention to build a competing legal-research product. That distinction — commercial intent to compete — is the analytical hinge a circuit court most likely uses to reconcile the two rulings.

"Ross's use is not transformative because it does not have a 'further purpose or different character' than Thomson Reuters's." — Judge Stephanos Bibas, Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence

Product intent as the fault line

The piece then brings in Jason Henderson of JWL International, who told TechCrunch that "they know that the AI model has been trained on so much stuff, and the law has not really caught up" and that "if what you're doing is training on somebody's property because your purpose is to directly compete, then the courts will frown on it." The formulation pushes the fault line onto product intent rather than the mechanics of ingestion. Silberling closes on the corollary from Thaler v. Perlmutter, in which the Copyright Office and the DC courts confirmed that a work generated entirely by an AI system without human authorial intervention is not eligible for US copyright protection. The industry now sits inside a paradox where the corpus feeding the models can be protected but the outputs of the trained models cannot.

"They know that the AI model has been trained on so much stuff, and the law has not really caught up." — Jason Henderson, Senior Attorney, JWL International
