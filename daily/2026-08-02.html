EU AI Act GPAI enforcement powers activate Sunday at 00:00 UTC with authority to fine 3 percent of global turnover

Twelve months after the Brussels regime for general-purpose AI models entered its obligations phase, the machinery to compel them turns on. The AI Office can request documentation, evaluate models directly, order corrective measures, restrict or withdraw models from the EU market, and impose fines up to €15 million or 3 percent of global annual turnover, whichever is higher, under Article 101. Article 50 transparency obligations activate the same moment, requiring machine-readable marking of AI-generated content and visible disclosure of deepfakes; the December 2, 2026 transition ends the grace period for systems that were already on the market before Sunday. Models placed before August 2, 2025 have until August 2, 2027 to bring themselves into compliance.

Techi · 2026-08-01 · Source ↗

Sunday, August 2, 2026 marks the moment the European Union's general-purpose AI enforcement powers cross from paper to practice. The obligations themselves have applied since August 2, 2025: providers of general-purpose AI models placed on the market after that date have been legally bound to publish training-content summaries, respect copyright, document their systems, and manage systemic risk. For twelve months, though, nobody in Brussels could compel any of it. That changes at 00:00 UTC.

From that moment, the AI Office at the European Commission can move from persuasion to compulsion: requesting documentation proving a model complies with its transparency and copyright duties, evaluating models directly, ordering corrective measures, restricting or withdrawing models from the EU market, and imposing fines. Article 101 of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 caps those fines at 3 percent of global annual turnover or €15 million, whichever is higher, for GPAI providers that intentionally or negligently infringe the regulation, fail to comply with a request for information, supply misleading information, fail to comply with a corrective measure, or fail to make a model available for evaluation. That floor is well above the €7.5 million cap that applies to smaller AI-Act violations under Article 99.

Two enforcement tracks, one activation moment

Article 50, the transparency chapter, activates in parallel. Providers of generative AI systems producing text, images, audio, or video must mark outputs in a machine-readable format detectable as artificially generated or manipulated. Deployers using AI to create deepfakes must disclose the artificial origin visibly to viewers. The Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content, published by the European Commission and AI Office on June 10, 2026, gives practical guidance on watermarking, metadata, and other detection methods. December 2, 2026 marks the end of the transitional period for systems already on the market before August 2, so pre-existing generative systems have four months to comply rather than needing to be retrofitted overnight.

Models placed on the market on or after August 2, 2025 are immediately subject to enforcement. Models placed before that date benefit from a longer runway, with providers having until August 2, 2027 to bring them into compliance. The staggered timing reflects the trade-off Brussels made when the AI Act passed: give incumbents a two-year path so the regime does not force expensive retraining of already-deployed systems, while making it clear that anything trained under the new rules must be born compliant.

The Code of Practice softens but does not block

Signing the GPAI Code of Practice softens penalty calculations and provides good-faith treatment, but does not block enforcement action. Providers that declined to sign the Code — Meta most publicly among the frontier labs — carry the full weight of statutory penalty exposure without the mitigation. The Commission has been explicit that the Code is a floor, not a shield: a signed Code participant can still be fined if it fails to actually meet the obligations the Code operationalizes.

"From August 2, the AI Office can demand documentation proving a model complies with its transparency and copyright duties." — Techi, EU AI Act GPAI Enforcement Begins August 2

Before adopting a fine decision, the Commission must communicate its preliminary findings to the provider and give it an opportunity to be heard. The Court of Justice of the European Union has unlimited jurisdiction to review, cancel, reduce, or increase any Article 101 fine on appeal.

Article 6 high-risk deadline also live

August 2, 2026 is also the binding enforcement date for high-risk AI system obligations under Articles 9 through 17 (provider requirements) and Article 26 (deployer requirements). Providers of AI systems used in Annex III categories — recruitment, credit scoring, education access, critical-infrastructure operation, law enforcement, migration, judicial administration, democratic processes — must have completed conformity assessments, finalized technical documentation, affixed CE marking, and registered in the EU database. A November 2025 European Commission proposal to defer certain high-risk deadlines to late 2027 was never adopted. Enterprises must treat Sunday as the operative deadline.

The activation lands after the largest publisher-side generative-AI copyright verdict on record: Anthropic's $1.5 billion Bartz settlement on July 28, and the Munich Regional Court's July 31 ruling that Suno's music-generation model infringed six GEMA-represented works — the first landmark European copyright verdict against a generative-AI vendor. GEMA CEO Tobias Holzmüller called that verdict "of global significance" and the timing, 36 hours before Sunday's enforcement machinery activates, was widely noted.

Big Picture

Sunday's midnight-UTC threshold is the largest single AI regulatory activation to date, converting a year of voluntary Code of Practice guidance into hard enforcement across the world's third-largest economy. Fines start at €15 million or 3 percent of global turnover under Article 101 for GPAI providers; Article 50 transparency obligations require every ChatGPT paragraph, every Muse image, every Suno track, and every Sora video sold into the EU to be machine-readably marked as AI, with deepfakes visibly labeled to viewers.

That clock expired Saturday. Treasury, the National Security Agency (via the Secretary of War), CISA (via the Secretary of Homeland Security) and NIST (via the Secretary of Commerce), in consultation with the White House Chief of Staff through the National Cyber Director and the Assistant to the President for Science and Technology, were directed to design a voluntary framework by August 1 under which frontier developers can engage the federal government prior to model release.

Two intertwined artifacts were due: a classified benchmarking process defining what counts as a "covered frontier model," and the voluntary early-access framework itself. The White House Office of the National Cyber Director distributed a draft framework to OpenAI, Anthropic and Google approximately two weeks ago; all three companies submitted joint revision proposals. Article 50 transparency obligations activate alongside GPAI enforcement, requiring machine-readable AI-content marking and visible deepfake disclosure

ComplianceHub · 2026-08-01 · Source ↗

Providers of generative AI systems producing text, images, audio, or video must mark outputs in a machine-readable format detectable as artificially generated, and deployers using AI to create deepfakes must disclose the artificial origin visibly. The Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content, published June 10, provides the practical guidance on watermarking and metadata. Systems already on the EU market before Sunday have a December 2, 2026 transitional deadline.

Article 50 of the EU AI Act activates in parallel with the GPAI enforcement powers at 00:00 UTC Sunday. It attaches transparency duties to both providers and deployers of generative AI systems and creates the first EU-wide requirement that content produced by an AI system carry a machine-readable mark indicating its provenance.

Two categories of obligation

For providers of generative AI systems producing synthetic audio, image, video or text content, the requirement is that outputs be marked in a machine-readable format and made detectable as artificially generated or manipulated. The Code of Practice published June 10 accepts several implementations: cryptographic watermarks embedded in image pixels or audio waveforms (Google's SynthID is the reference implementation), C2PA content credentials, and file-level metadata. OpenAI's July 31 rollout of SynthID watermarking on GPT-Live audio through ChatGPT Voice and the OpenAI API was widely read as an Article-50 compliance ship at the deadline.

For deployers using AI to create deepfakes — an image, audio or video that appreciably resembles existing persons, objects, places or events and would falsely appear to a person to be authentic — a visible disclosure to viewers is required. The disclosure must be legible, apparent and not defeated by ordinary content transformation. The Code of Practice contemplates persistent visual overlays, opening-frame text disclosures, and metadata tagging as complementary requirements.

Providers of AI systems that generate or manipulate text with the purpose of informing the public on matters of public interest carry the strongest additional duty: labeling that the text has been artificially generated or manipulated, with a narrow exception where the AI-generated content has undergone a human review or editorial control process and a natural or legal person holds editorial responsibility.

Transition periods and enforcement path

Systems already placed on the EU market before Sunday get a transitional grace period until December 2, 2026 to bring their marking into compliance. Systems put into service after Sunday must be compliant from day one. The Commission has been explicit that the December 2 date is not a soft deadline: after that day, non-compliant pre-existing systems face the same enforcement exposure as noncompliant new systems.

Article 50 violations are penalized under Article 99, with fines up to €7.5 million or 1 percent of global annual turnover, whichever is higher. That is smaller than the Article 101 exposure that applies to GPAI providers but still substantial. Enforcement is national-competent-authority-led rather than Commission-led, meaning each Member State's AI supervisory authority will make the specific enforcement decisions.

The law passed unanimously in both chambers of the Illinois General Assembly earlier in July and takes effect on January 1, 2028. That two-year gap is deliberate: it gives platforms and the Illinois Attorney General's office time to build the enforcement infrastructure the statute requires.

What the law actually does

The law bans targeted algorithmic content feeds for Illinois users under 18. It disables algorithm-driven notifications between 10 pm and 7 am. Direct messages from friends and loved ones are exempted from the notification blackout, which the drafters described as an intentional carve-out for social communication rather than platform-driven engagement bait.

Age verification is required at the device operating-system layer rather than the app layer. This is the newer regulatory pattern, first used in the Australian equivalent, that shifts the compliance burden from each individual social platform to Apple and Google. The consequence is that a jailbroken or side-loaded device likely evades the verification, and that operating-system providers pick up the age-signaling responsibility they previously refused to accept.

Stronger default privacy settings for users under 18 include location-sharing limits, restricted DMs from non-contacts, and mandatory pause and screen-time reminders after set intervals. Civil penalties are set at $50,000 per violation, enforced by the Illinois Attorney General. Class-action provisions were stripped from the final draft during the legislative committee.

"Big social media companies have intentionally designed their platforms to keep kids online for as long as possible." — Governor JB Pritzker

Illinois is the eighth state to enact this class of legislation, following Utah, California, Louisiana, New York, Florida, Texas, and Minnesota. The Illinois version is the strictest so far on the algorithmic-feed side and the first to move age verification down to the operating-system layer as a matter of statutory design rather than as a compliance suggestion. The 2028 effective date lands roughly six months after the EU AI Act's high-risk provisions covering algorithmic content recommendation for minors will have gone through their first full enforcement cycle, so the Illinois AG's office will have EU precedent to lean on when the statute goes live.
