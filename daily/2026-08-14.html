Anthropic in talks to buy Israeli inference and video startup Decart for $6 billion

Bloomberg broke the deal Thursday morning: Anthropic is negotiating what would be, by a wide margin, its largest acquisition ever, paying roughly $6 billion for a three-year-old Israeli startup whose real-time video generation, world models and GPU-optimization stack would fold into Anthropic's inference and performance group. The price marks a 50 percent step-up from Decart's May round in under three months, arrives one week after Anthropic's $9.1 billion 20-year Rockdale lease, and slots the deal alongside four prior 2026 acquisitions as the lab moves from renting capacity to owning capability ahead of an anticipated IPO.

Bloomberg / Fortune · 2026-08-13 · Source ↗

Bloomberg published the Anthropic and Decart talks at roughly 08:40 New York time Thursday, with Fortune, PYMNTS and Quartz all matching the story within the hour. Anthropic is in advanced discussions to acquire Decart, an Israeli startup founded in 2023 by Dean Leitersdorf, his brother Orian Leitersdorf and Moshe Shalev, for approximately $6 billion. The deal is not final and could still fall through. Both companies declined to comment on the record. If it closes it would be Anthropic's largest known acquisition to date and the lab's fifth acquisition of 2026, following a run of smaller tuck-ins including Vercept earlier this year.

The price marks a step-up of roughly 50 percent from Decart's May 2026 round, which valued the company at $4 billion on a $300 million investment led by Radical Ventures with Nvidia, Toyota, Atreides Management, Valor Equity Partners and Adobe Ventures participating. The August 2025 round pegged the company at $3.1 billion, so the May-to-August 2026 window compresses a doubling of paper value into three months. Decart employs about 100 people. Dean Leitersdorf and Shalev are both alumni of the Israel Defense Forces' Unit 8200 signals-intelligence organization, the same pipeline that produced Wiz and a long list of prior enterprise-security exits.

"This would be, by a wide margin, Anthropic's biggest known acquisition." — Bloomberg reporting summary, Aug 13

Two capabilities Anthropic wants under one roof

Decart's product portfolio splits into two halves that map onto two of Anthropic's most acute operational headaches. The first is DOS, Decart's training and inference optimization platform, which the company says lowers AI training costs by improving chip-utilization efficiency. Anthropic has been running the highest sustained inference load in the frontier-lab tier for eighteen months, and its August disclosures put the company at a $30 billion revenue run rate versus $9 billion at the end of 2025, which pulls GPU-hour cost from an operations line into a strategic priority. Folding Decart's DOS team into Anthropic's inference and performance group is the piece Bloomberg's sources named explicitly.

The second half is the world-model stack. Decart's Lucy model generates real-time, modifiable interactive video from live feeds. It has been used by streamers on Twitch, TikTok and YouTube, including virtual creator CodeMiko, to render synthetic clothing try-ons and other in-stream video effects. eBay is both a Decart customer and an investor. Oasis 3, released in June 2026, generates simulated environments for training robotics and autonomous-driving systems. Anthropic does not currently ship a video-generation product. OpenAI has Sora and Google has Veo. A $6 billion acquisition of Decart's Lucy and Oasis stack closes that capability gap in a single transaction rather than through the multi-year in-house build the two rivals ran.

"I really can't imagine being in any other market. This market is moving so fast." — Dean Leitersdorf, Decart CEO, in earlier public comments cited by Fortune

Where it fits in the wider Anthropic capex arc

The deal lands one week after Anthropic signed a $9.1 billion 20-year lease with Riot Platforms for 191 megawatts at Rockdale, Texas, potentially $16.1 billion with extensions. It sits alongside standing compute agreements with AWS, Google, SpaceX and Meta. Anthropic has filed no S-1 but has been widely described as preparing for an IPO. Reuters and The Information have both reported preliminary conversations with underwriters for a listing in 2027. In that context the Decart purchase is Anthropic buying inputs that public-market investors read as durable moat: proprietary inference tooling that reduces cost of goods sold, a video-generation capability that opens a new revenue lane, and a team of 100 that includes the DOS engineers who wrote the optimization stack in the first place.

SpaceX was previously in acquisition discussions with Decart earlier in 2026, according to reports Elon Musk publicly denied at the time. The Anthropic bid, at $6 billion, is presumed higher than any prior offer. TechCrunch's reporting flagged that Anthropic and OpenAI have both been running quiet capability tuck-ins across coding, agents and now video, and that the Decart price sets a public reference for how much a frontier lab will pay to buy rather than build the next capability layer. The $6 billion figure, if it holds, will likely reset the term-sheet floor for other AI-video and inference-optimization companies still on the outside of a strategic transaction.

Two open questions remain. First, whether Israeli regulatory review under Israel's Committee for Foreign Investments will impose any structural conditions on a US-buyer deal for an IDF-veteran-founded startup. Second, whether Decart's existing investor cap table, which includes both Nvidia and Toyota, will complicate a full sale versus a partial acquihire. Bloomberg's sources framed both risks as manageable. Neither Anthropic nor Decart provided any public timing target for closing.

Anthropic flips Claude Code auto mode to default across Pro, Max and Team plans

Anthropic · 2026-08-14 · Source ↗

New sessions no longer prompt per-command approval. A classifier screens tool calls and blocks irreversible, destructive or out-of-environment actions. Anthropic's 1,053-tester study says the classifier catches 89 percent of dangerous commands versus 13.6 percent for humans on the first try and 5 percent after 50 prompts.

Anthropic flipped Claude Code's default permission mode to fully automated on Friday, August 14, across Pro, Max and Team plans. The old flow, which surfaced an approval prompt for every shell command a Claude Code session wanted to run, has been replaced by an inline classifier that screens tool calls and blocks anything irreversible, destructive or aimed outside the user environment. Deny rules and explicit ask rules configured by the user or repository administrator continue to evaluate before the classifier and still block or prompt as before. Anthropic disclosed that classifier overhead no longer counts toward Pro, Max or Team usage limits as of the flip.

The evidence Anthropic published to justify the default change is a controlled 1,053-tester user study conducted internally. In the study, the classifier caught 89 percent of dangerous commands. Human approvers caught a dangerous command 13.6 percent of the time on their first try and dropped to roughly 5 percent by their fiftieth prompt, a decline Anthropic explicitly attributed to approval fatigue. Users who previously set a custom default receive a one-time in-product prompt asking if they want to switch to auto mode. The switch remains user-controllable at any time.

"In testing, it caught 89 percent of dangerous commands. Manual approval caught 14 percent." — Anthropic Claude Code team, on X

Independent reaction and the safety-review shift

The change arrived three days after Anthropic tagged Claude Code v2.1.229 with a Windows Git detection fix and stream-idle timeout hardening. It preceded the v2.1.231 hot-fix that patched an MCP OAuth failure blocking Slack sign-in. Reddit's r/ClaudeAI and Hacker News threads reacted along a predictable axis: developers who watch every prompt approve of the fatigue framing; developers who use Claude Code for delegated multi-hour sessions were less enthusiastic about the shift of dangerous-command review from the user's judgment to the model provider's classifier. Anthropic's blog post frames the change as a data-driven improvement over approval fatigue rather than a shift of safety responsibility.

"Testers caught a dangerous command 13.6 percent of the time, and closer to 5 percent after 50 prompts." — Anthropic 1,053-tester user study
