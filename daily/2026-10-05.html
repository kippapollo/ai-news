Trump names Clayton to chair a federal Super Intelligence Force. The order renames AI.

The Truth Social post went up Sunday afternoon. DNI Jay Clayton chairs; FTC Chair Andrew Ferguson, Undersecretary of War for Research and Engineering Emil Michael, and OPM Director Scott Kupor are vice chairs. The group has 120 days to produce a report on risks and opportunities and operates under a mandate to "prevent overregulation and regulatory capture that would stifle innovation and competition." Policy · Trend · TechCrunch · 2026-10-04 · Source ↗

President Trump announced the Super Intelligence Force in a Truth Social post Sunday afternoon, naming Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton chair of the body and three vice chairs: Federal Trade Commission Chair Andrew Ferguson, Undersecretary of War for Research and Engineering Emil Michael, and Office of Personnel Management Director Scott Kupor. The group reports to Trump and Chief of Staff Susie Wiles. Its charter, as reported by TechCrunch and NBC News, gives it 120 days to deliver a report on the risks and opportunities presented by AI and directs it to "develop plans for responding to SI-enabled threats to our society, while preventing overregulation and regulatory capture that would stifle innovation and competition." The force is tasked with coordinating federal engagement across consumers, public-interest groups, religious organizations, infrastructure providers, and AI companies.

In the same post, Trump signed an executive order rebranding "artificial intelligence" as "super intelligence" across federal entities. The order carries the renaming into agency communications, budget documents, and reporting. The announcement follows a September Trump statement sketching the task-force concept and announcing a czar position. Elon Musk confirmed hours later on X that SpaceXAI, the Grok-serving subsidiary carved out of xAI, will rebrand to SpaceXSI to align with the federal nomenclature shift.

"Super Intelligence is bigger than the Industrial Revolution, and the internet, and will protect the interests, and improve the lives, of all Americans." — President Donald Trump, Truth Social announcement, 2026-10-04

Who's on it

Clayton is the ninth Director of National Intelligence, sworn in in August 2026 after Tulsi Gabbard's departure, and chaired the Securities and Exchange Commission under the first Trump administration from 2017 to 2020. Clayton has publicly argued the United States risks falling behind competitors such as China if it retreats from AI development. Ferguson, chairing the FTC since 2025, opens a federal investigation lane that was already active following the September OpenAI agent-breach probe. Michael, from the War Department research-and-engineering office, oversees Project Meridian, the 120-day warfighting study announced the same day. Kupor, the Office of Personnel Management director and former general partner at a16z, is the lone venture-capital alumnus of the group.

What it isn't

The force is not legislative; it does not create new statutory authority. It is explicitly ordered to coordinate across federal agencies rather than impose binding standards. The 120-day report is a deliverable to the White House, not Congress. The group also sits alongside, not above, the SAFA (Standards Authority for Frontier AI) proposal that Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic are advancing privately with a FINRA-style self-regulatory model.

What stakeholders said

Reactions were muted on the record. Clayton's prior public remarks framed the task as competitive positioning. Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google did not comment within hours of the post. The AI-safety advocacy wing had already queued the #TeamHuman campaign that launched the same weekend, which calls for a global slowdown through international agreements and tracks specialized AI chips as nuclear-grade regulated goods; the force's charter language on "preventing overregulation" directly rebuts that proposal. California Attorney General Rob Bonta's October 1 subpoena of OpenAI continues to run on a separate track. Several House and Senate aides contacted after the announcement said staff is awaiting the 120-day report before committing to markups on pending AI-liability legislation.

"The Super Intelligence Force is tasked with developing plans for responding to SI-enabled threats to our society, while preventing overregulation and regulatory capture." — Super Intelligence Force charter language

The rename

The executive-order rename takes effect across federal entity communications; agencies have 30 days to update official documents and 90 days to migrate budget-line nomenclature. The practical effect in the first week is public-facing: the National Institute of Standards and Technology's AI Risk Management Framework becomes the SI Risk Management Framework; the Office of Science and Technology Policy's Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights sits for revision; the Pentagon's responsible-AI directive, DoD Directive 3000.09, retains its number but changes its subject line. Industry adoption is voluntary. OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google continue to use "artificial intelligence" in product and research communications. Musk's SpaceXSI rename is, so far, the only major private alignment to the federal shift.

Google starts rolling out Gemini 4 Argon through the Fairwind Program.

CNBC · 2026-10-04 · Source ↗

Google's first frontier model since Gemini 3 posts 77.9 percent on DeepSWE v1.1, a tie-for-first 68 percent on CWE-bench, and a 1-million-token output ceiling. Cybersecurity partners get first access; paid API and AI Ultra subscribers follow.

Google unveiled Gemini 4 Argon on September 30 and started rolling it out through the Fairwind Program this week, with Sunday coverage from CNBC and The Decoder adding the first independent benchmark reads. On DeepSWE v1.1 the model scores 77.9 percent, placing it in the top three alongside Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Sol. On CWE-bench it ties at 68 percent with Opus 5.5. The output ceiling is 1 million tokens; introductory pricing is $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, below Opus 5.5's and GPT-6 Sol's post-release pricing.

Argon's distinguishing design choice is a staged release through cybersecurity partners before general availability. The Fairwind Program hands early access to vetted defenders working with Google on vulnerability discovery and triage, with the paid API and AI Ultra subscriptions scheduled to follow. The sequencing follows the Anthropic Mythos launch earlier in the quarter and reflects a growing convention that frontier models go to defenders before offense-capable actors can commodify them. Google's AI principles update, published alongside the launch, lists pre-release cyber-capability testing as a required step for all frontier models.

"We're letting defenders stress-test what the model can do before we open the gates." — Google, Fairwind Program announcement

Capability targets

Argon targets long-horizon reasoning, agentic coding, enterprise knowledge work, and cybersecurity. The 1-million-token output ceiling matches the input-context mark Google hit with Gemini 4 Preview on Titan-A sparse attention, meaning the model can emit multi-hundred-thousand-token traces without truncation. Google positioned the model against GPT-6 Sol and Opus 5.5 directly; internal reports surfaced employee skepticism about whether real-world coding productivity matches the benchmark numbers, with some engineers questioning the "frontier" label management applied.

Pricing and competition

Argon's $2/$10 pricing undercuts Opus 5.5 ($5/$25) and GPT-6 Sol ($4/$20) by roughly half on output, continuing the price compression that started with the September 22 Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Sol launches. The compression is driven in part by Chinese open-weight pressure. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, Alibaba's Qwen 4 lineup, and Moonshot's Kimi K2 all now sit at pricing levels that force frontier labs to defend margin at the output tier. Argon's Fairwind-first rollout buys Google a quarter of safety signal before volume demand forces infrastructure provisioning.

The caveat

Argon has not been tested publicly against OSWorld 2.0, the computer-use benchmark GPT-6 Astra led at 72.6 last week. Google has committed to publishing OSWorld numbers before the paid-API rollout, but as of Sunday night the data was not posted. Argon is also not yet classified at the Preparedness Framework's Critical cyber tier; Google's internal eval places it one notch below. Anthropic's Frontier Red Team has not independently certified Argon.

PolicySafety

Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic file for a FINRA-style frontier-AI standards body.

The Next Web · 2026-10-04 · Source ↗

The Standards Authority for Frontier AI operates without government oversight and targets a 2027 launch. The three companies approached Sriram Krishnan to serve as CEO. Microsoft, Meta, and xAI are not in the founding group.

Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic have moved past the private-draft stage of the Standards Authority for Frontier AI, with The Information and The Next Web publishing details Sunday of a formal proposal to launch SAFA by end of 2026 or early 2027. The body is modeled on FINRA, the US securities self-regulatory organization, which gives it broad standard-setting power without government control. The three companies approached former White House AI policy adviser Sriram Krishnan to serve as CEO; Arati Prabhakar, former OSTP director, was contacted for a leadership role.

SAFA's charter covers three areas. First, pre-release model testing: common protocols for evaluating cyber capability, bio-uplift, autonomy, and persuasion before a frontier model ships. Second, safety-incident reporting: a shared repository where member labs disclose containment breaches, jailbreaks that reach customer data, and agent misbehavior requiring intervention. Third, auditor accreditation: certifying outside firms that may audit lab practices, model safety cases, and incident response. The three areas mirror the structure proposed by Anthropic in its March 2026 white paper on frontier regulation.

"Self-regulation is not enough for AI." — Bill Gates, in prior public remarks on frontier-model governance

What's not in it

Microsoft, Meta, and xAI are absent from the founding group. Microsoft's absence is the most consequential: Microsoft is OpenAI's primary infrastructure partner and sits on the Preparedness Framework reviewer list. The company has not publicly explained its non-participation. Meta's absence tracks with its decision to publish open weights, which complicates participation in a pre-release testing regime. xAI's absence is consistent with Elon Musk's public opposition to industry self-regulation that he does not control.

The shift

The three companies initially preferred federal oversight, which stalled when a draft White House executive order on frontier-AI testing failed to clear the Trump administration this summer. The SAFA pivot reflects the lab's assessment that no federal regime is coming in this term. Trump's Super Intelligence Force, announced the same day, carries an explicit mandate against overregulation that reinforces the lab's calculus.

Critics

Former Meta executive Nick Clegg told reporters this week that "AI labs cannot keep regulating themselves." Bill Gates has argued self-regulation is insufficient. The Center for AI Safety and signatories of the #TeamHuman campaign that launched the same weekend favor an IAEA-style intergovernmental body over an industry self-regulator. SAFA's response has been that FINRA's authority over broker-dealers demonstrates that an SRO can impose binding rules and sanctions. The weakness in the comparison is that FINRA's rules bind brokerage firms, which must register with the SEC; SAFA has no equivalent registration gate.

Altman: "a lot of daylight" between OpenAI and Anthropic on risk.

Yahoo Finance / Politico Decoded · 2026-10-04 · Source ↗

In Politico's Decoded newsletter, Altman distinguished OpenAI's willingness to accept "some bad things happening for the benefits of this technology" from Anthropic's preference for concentrated safeguards. The framing lands two weeks after Anthropic's S-1 risk-factor section.

Sam Altman told Politico's Decoded newsletter in an October 4 interview that there is "a lot of daylight" between OpenAI and Anthropic on AI risk and regulation. Altman framed OpenAI's position as acceptance of individual misuse and localized harm in exchange for broad access and user agency. "We believe that the world should accept some bad things happening for the benefits of this technology and people having the agency," he said. Altman distinguished that posture from catastrophic loss-of-control scenarios, which he said OpenAI treats as a hard constraint.

The interview lands two weeks after Anthropic's September 26 S-1, which listed "existential risks to humanity" among its risk factors, and 15 days after Dario Amodei's "We Must Pace the Frontier" essay drew public endorsement from Altman, Hassabis, Musk, and Nadella. Altman's reaffirmation of the common ground on catastrophic risk is paired with a sharper public split on everyday risk. The disagreement maps onto the policy positions the two companies take on California's SB 53 and on the SAFA incident-reporting threshold.

"We believe that the world should accept some bad things happening for the benefits of this technology and people having the agency." — Sam Altman, OpenAI CEO, Politico Decoded, 2026-10-04

The daylight on specifics

Where OpenAI and Anthropic diverge publicly: deployment speed (OpenAI faster, Anthropic slower), access breadth (OpenAI consumer-wide; Anthropic enterprise and developer-first), external pre-release testing (Anthropic opts in to Frontier Red Team work with the UK AISI and US AISI; OpenAI has not committed to the same for every frontier release), and model-weights security (Anthropic publishes its ASL framework; OpenAI's Preparedness Framework is comparable but less publicly committed to pausing on specific thresholds). Altman did not address the question of model-weight exfiltration risk directly, which has been Anthropic's loudest public concern since the Hacktron breach.

The context

The interview comes four days after a federal grand jury seated by the Northern District of California opened its own OpenAI probe following Bonta's state-level October 1 subpoena. The Ferguson-led FTC inquiry from September 30 remains active. Altman's willingness to publicly split from Anthropic on tolerable-risk framing, after months of united messaging, reflects both companies' recognition that regulatory posture is diverging and that the SAFA proposal does not resolve the disagreement.

YouTube creators and Nobel laureates launch #TeamHuman for a global AI slowdown.

TeamHuman.org · 2026-10-04 · Source ↗

Mark Rober, Kurzgesagt, Rebecca Zamolo, Preston, and The Infographics Show headline a 60-creator slate. Hinton and Bengio co-sign. The pledge calls for chip tracking, international speed limits, and an IAEA-style AI watchdog.

#TeamHuman went live Sunday morning as a public pledge calling for a global slowdown of advanced AI development through international agreements. The campaign is led by YouTube creators representing over 300 million combined subscribers, with Mark Rober (83.1 million), Kurzgesagt (25.6 million), Rebecca Zamolo (23.3 million), Preston (17.9 million), and The Infographics Show (15.5 million) heading a 60-plus creator slate. The Center for AI Safety is the institutional sponsor. Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio co-signed; additional signatories include Nobel economist Daron Acemoglu, former Joint Chiefs chair Mike Mullen, Stephen Fry, and Prince Harry and Meghan.

The pledge language is tight: "AI is moving faster than our ability to control it. Alongside a chorus of experts, we call for a global slowdown through international agreements." Two policy proposals follow. "Build the brakes" tracks specialized AI chips the same way nuclear and bioweapon precursors are tracked, restricting when and where new frontier training runs can begin. "Enforce a speed limit" calls for international agreements among major powers under an IAEA-model watchdog that can inspect training clusters and incident reports.

"AI is moving faster than our ability to control it. Alongside a chorus of experts, we call for a global slowdown through international agreements." — #TeamHuman pledge language, teamhuman.org

Campaign mechanics

The organizers scheduled a fall delivery of signatures to lawmakers in Washington and Brussels. The campaign cites a 68 percent voter-backing figure for a pause on advanced AI development and superintelligence restrictions, drawn from a Center for AI Safety-commissioned poll run in September. Signature counts were not disclosed at launch; the site collects email addresses and verification is not public.

The timing

The launch date is deliberate. Sunday overlaps Trump's Super Intelligence Force announcement; the campaign framing — regulated chip tracking, international watchdog — is the exact framing the force's charter language rebuts as "overregulation and regulatory capture." The campaign also lands as SAFA, the industry self-regulatory proposal from Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic, is advancing. #TeamHuman's position is that an industry-run standards body is not sufficient; a government-run or intergovernmental regime is required.

What's new

This is the first mass-creator coalition in AI safety. Prior advocacy was researcher-led (the April 2023 FLI letter, the July 2026 1,200-signatory pacing letter). The 300-million-subscriber reach matters because it is the first time AI-safety messaging has a direct distribution channel comparable to the one labs use to launch models. The campaign's effectiveness will be measured over the quarter in whether specific legislative proposals, especially California's SB 53 renewal and the EU AI Act Article 51 revision, adopt the chip-tracking language.

Schneider Electric is in advanced discussions to acquire US engineering software company PTC for more than $20 billion, Bloomberg reported Sunday night. PTC's portfolio is the integration prize. Creo is PTC's parametric CAD system, used in automotive, aerospace, and heavy-industrial design. ThingWorx is PTC's industrial IoT platform. The four together give Schneider a vertical stack that reaches from factory-floor sensors through product lifecycle data into design software.

"Schneider nears a $20 billion deal that would combine PTC's product design, lifecycle management, and industrial IoT with Schneider's energy and automation infrastructure." — Bloomberg, 2026-10-04

The deal is being pitched to investors as an AI play. Schneider's energy-management customers include a large share of the hyperscaler data centers now being built out. Folding PTC's product-lifecycle and IoT data into Schneider's energy stack creates the kind of integrated-operations surface Siemens and Rockwell are also building; both are expected to respond with counter-bids or alternative tie-ups.

Risk factors

EU antitrust review is the gating risk. Schneider is already a dominant player in industrial controls across Europe; adding PTC's CAD and IoT share raises overlap questions in verticals including automotive and aerospace. Anthropic recorded more than $660 million in non-cash employee charity-match expense between October 2025 and March 2026, with Q1 2026 alone running at roughly $125 million. The figure is drawn from a The Information review of the S-1 and investor materials circulating ahead of Anthropic's planned November roadshow. Charity-match is Anthropic's program to match employee donations to AI-safety organizations with Anthropic equity. The $125 million Q1 2026 number is approximately 10 percent of employee expense and 2 percent of operating cost in the quarter.

The program's post-IPO trajectory is where the number gets interesting. Anthropic expects charity-match expense to run into the billions in the two years after the IPO, as employees cash in the matched equity awards. The matched shares are granted at valuation reference points that will mark up substantially after the proposed $2 trillion-plus IPO. The accounting treatment is non-cash — the equity dilutes existing holders rather than draining the balance sheet — but it is real expense on the P&L. Anthropic plans to exclude the figure from the adjusted-profit measure it will show investors, a treatment some analysts have flagged.

"The charity-match expense is non-cash. Whether investors should discount it from adjusted profit is the live question." — The Information, 2026-10-04

Where the money goes

The matched donations flow to AI-safety organizations, including the Center for AI Safety, METR, Apollo Research, and the Alignment Research Center. The $660 million six-month figure gives those organizations a cash-flow footprint comparable to several hyperscaler philanthropy programs; it is likely the largest single-source non-governmental AI-safety funding pipeline in operation.

The investor read

Anthropic's defense of the adjusted-profit exclusion is that the expense is a one-time step-up tied to the IPO-adjacent mark; it will normalize at a lower run rate once equity grants stabilize. Analysts contacted after The Information published argued the better treatment is to include the full stock-based compensation line, which rolls up the charity match, in adjusted-profit and let investors see the dilution.

"There is no way to avoid the attack." — Kim Yo Jong, state media, 2026-10-04

Chollet: OpenAI's LLM concentration set AGI back five to ten years.

TechRadar Pro / Yahoo Tech · 2026-10-04 · Source ↗

ARC Prize co-founder François Chollet argues OpenAI's funding concentration on LLMs closed down frontier-research publishing and crowded alternative architectures out of the field.

François Chollet, Keras's creator and ARC Prize co-founder, argued in a podcast interview released Sunday that OpenAI "basically set back progress to AGI by five to 10 years." His case is that OpenAI's concentration of funding and talent on large language models closed down frontier-research publishing in the industry, pulled researchers away from alternative architectures, and crowded out approaches that might have reached general reasoning sooner. "LLMs sucked the oxygen out of the room," Chollet said. He credits OpenAI with triggering the initial hype burst that commercialized the field but blames the follow-on effect for narrowing the field.

Chollet's position has been consistent since the 2024 ARC Prize launch. He argues LLMs are a dead-end for AGI even as they become more powerful on specific tasks. The ARC Prize's $1 million award for solving ARC-AGI-2 is his instrument for redirecting research. His comments this week are notable not for being new but for landing in the same window as Dario Amodei's pacing essay, the SAFA proposal, and the Trump Super Intelligence Force announcement — each of which treats "frontier LLM" as the stable reference for frontier AI.

"LLMs sucked the oxygen out of the room." — François Chollet, ARC Prize co-founder

The specific critique

Chollet names three mechanisms. First, closed research publishing: OpenAI's GPT-3 moment pushed the field toward trade-secret posture, with Anthropic, Google DeepMind, and Meta FAIR all cutting back on paper publication by late 2023. Second, talent concentration: the frontier labs vacuumed up researchers who might otherwise have run alternative architectures. Third, benchmark lock-in: evaluation suites skewed toward tasks LLMs happen to do well, which further narrowed what counted as progress.

Dissent

The direct responses within the field have been mixed. Rich Sutton has long argued that scaling and compute are the dominant driver and that architecture concerns are secondary; he dismissed the five-to-ten-year claim as unquantifiable. Yann LeCun agreed with the direction of the critique but not the magnitude. Several Anthropic researchers pushed back privately that the alignment and interpretability work the field has done on LLMs will transfer to any next architecture; the time was not wasted.

Strata runs a 125-billion-parameter Qwen on a 12-gigabyte consumer GPU at 60–95 t/s.

Strata / GitHub · 2026-10-04 · Source ↗

The open-source engine activates 10 of 24,576 experts per token and streams speculative drafting on top. Target hardware: 12 GB VRAM plus 64 GB system RAM.

Strata, an open-source inference engine released Sunday, runs Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, a 125-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model, on a single consumer GPU with 12 gigabytes of VRAM and 64 gigabytes of system RAM at 60 to 95 tokens per second. The engine's two technical levers are expert routing and speculative drafting. Qwen3.8-Flash-Next's MoE has 24,576 experts; Strata activates only 10 per token. The engine streams a drafter alongside the main execution path and claims a 1.6 to 1.8x speedup over conventional MoE serving on comparable hardware.

The result matters because it collapses the hardware floor for serving a top-tier open-weights model from eight-H100 clusters to a mid-range gaming rig. "We're not training a new model. We're changing what single-GPU inference can carry." — Strata technical post, 2026-10-04

How it works

The engine keeps the full MoE expert pool on system RAM and streams only the activated experts into VRAM per layer. Speculative drafting uses a 2-billion-parameter sibling as the drafter; verifier passes run on the full model. The expert-selection path uses a cached-approximation router that misses the top-10 selection less than 2 percent of the time on standard benchmarks, with a correction pass when it does. The engine is Apache 2.0 licensed and ships with pre-built binaries for Linux, macOS (Apple Silicon), and Windows.

Caveats

60–95 tokens per second is prompt-length dependent; the engine's throughput drops at long contexts because the speculative drafter struggles to keep ahead on complex reasoning traces.
