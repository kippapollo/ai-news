Anthropic IPO scrutiny sharpens: investors demand revenue-per-token, Motley Fool warns $2T listing would set a bubble bar

The Information reported Sunday that prospective Anthropic IPO investors are asking the company to disclose two metrics no AI company has published in a public offering before: revenue per token and revenue per gigawatt of committed compute. Motley Fool's Sean Williams published a Sep 7 piece calling a rumored $2 trillion listing "above 30 times sales" and pulled Truist data showing tech IPOs since mid-2012 have averaged a 55 percent maximum drawdown in year one. Anthropic filed confidentially on June 1, is targeting a late September to early October listing per multiple bank readouts, and posted Q2 sales above $11.5 billion up more than 1,300 percent year over year with a small adjusted profit. What Anthropic discloses will set the reporting template every downstream AI listing gets measured against.

Economic · Tech · The Information / Motley Fool · 2026-09-07 · Source ↗

Sean Williams's Motley Fool column posted at 3:33 AM PT Sep 7 walks through three historical parallels Williams argues should give a public-market buyer pause. First: the Truist Financial dataset covering tech-sector IPOs from mid-2012 through 2024, which shows an average first-year maximum drawdown of 55 percent and a median of 54. Second: Anthropic at the widely leaked $2 trillion valuation would price at approximately 30 times its Q2 annualized sales, a ratio Williams argues has never survived past three years in tech even for genuinely transformative companies. Third: every technology cycle since the 1990s internet boom that reached this scale of retail participation ended with a valuation correction inside twenty-four months of the peak. The column is a piece of financial-media opinion rather than research, but its publication tracks a broader Sunday-evening tightening of the narrative around the offering.

The Information's Sunday enterprise reporting is the sharper development. According to a piece Aaron Holmes posted Sunday afternoon that quickly circulated on X, prospective institutional investors have started asking Anthropic's bankers for two disclosures the company has not yet committed to include in its S-1: revenue per token consumed by paying users, and revenue per gigawatt of contracted compute. Neither number appears in the standard SaaS-metrics disclosure playbook, and neither has been published by any AI company that has raised public capital to date. The ask is a direct response to Anthropic's disclosed 14.8 gigawatts of contracted compute against $517 billion of ten-year commitments; investors want a defensible unit-economics number they can compare against OpenAI and Google when those companies eventually publish their own.

"What Anthropic discloses will set a benchmark for OpenAI and other AI companies, and could give investors much clearer insight into a business that's grown extraordinarily fast." — The Information, Sep 7, 2026, on the revenue-per-token disclosure ask

What the numbers look like right now

Anthropic filed confidentially on June 1, according to Forge Global's IPO tracker, following a Series H-1 that valued the company at approximately $965 billion. Recent secondary-market pricing implied a valuation of $1.4 to $1.6 trillion. The New York Times reported in late August that bankers had begun quoting a target range of $2 trillion, which would make Anthropic the largest technology IPO in US market history and roughly ten times the size of Alibaba's 2014 debut. The offering window Anthropic's team has been briefing is a late September to early October debut, subject to market conditions and the September FOMC decision.

The Q2 financial disclosures are unusual for a pre-IPO filing. Anthropic reported Q2 sales north of $11.5 billion, up more than 1,300 percent year over year, with a small adjusted profit. The revenue base is heavily API-weighted; ChatGPT-style consumer subscription revenue is a minority. Gross margins, according to a summary The Information published in August, dropped from about 50 percent to about 40 percent as inference costs on the Sol-competing frontier stack ran higher than modeled. That gross-margin compression is the second data point IPO buyers are pushing back on. Krishna Rao, Anthropic's CFO, told bankers the compression is temporary and reverses when the Google TPU capacity ramps through Q1 2027; investors want to see the sensitivity math.

The revenue-per-token, revenue-per-gigawatt argument

The reason a revenue-per-token number would matter is that it lets investors decouple two things that currently trade under one number. A token generated by a Fable 5.1 coding-agent inference at $75 per million output tokens on the Enterprise API produces one revenue-per-token figure. A token generated inside the Claude.ai consumer product with a $20 monthly subscription produces a completely different figure, because subscription revenue is amortized over an unpredictable token count. Anthropic's current SaaS-style disclosures collapse the two into a single revenue line. Prospective institutional investors, per The Information, want to see the coding-agent-per-token economics separated from the consumer-subscription-per-token economics so they can model the gross-margin evolution under different mixes of the two.

Revenue per gigawatt is the harder ask. A gigawatt of contracted compute at Anthropic's disclosed $517 billion / 14.8 GW average implies about $35 billion of ten-year commitment per GW, and buyers want to see revenue per GW as a diligence check on whether the utilization assumptions in the compute contracts are realistic. The disclosure would set a public template that OpenAI's eventual filing would have to match or explain around. Ilya Sutskever's Safe Superintelligence is understood to be paying attention to the Anthropic reporting choices because SSI's own investor stack is preparing similar diligence.

"At a $2 trillion valuation, Anthropic would trade above 30 times sales. That is a price-to-sales ratio companies at the forefront of game-changing technologies have not been able to sustain, historically." — Sean Williams, The Motley Fool, Sep 7, 2026

What comes next

The public S-1 filing is now widely expected in the two weeks following the September FOMC meeting on September 16. If Anthropic clears revenue-per-token and revenue-per-gigawatt disclosures in the S-1, the reporting template will apply pressure to Ilya Sutskever's SSI and to OpenAI's eventual filing. If Anthropic refuses either disclosure, book-building may run into resistance from the exact institutional investors the offering is priced to attract. Motley Fool's Williams closes his column with a two-line hedge: he acknowledges Anthropic's technology and market position are legitimate, and says he still would not participate in the IPO on the historical parallels alone. The next tell will be whether the two sell-side analysts on his critique list, Bank of America's Justin Post and Morgan Stanley's Brian Nowak, publish initiation notes at the price talk range in the two weeks after the S-1 files.

Google DeepMind MaxKernel closes the TPU kernel gap: 2.32× over reference code, 7.85× on DeepSeek V4 prefill

arXiv 2609.04523 / Hugging Face Papers · 2026-09-07 · Source ↗

Nine Google authors described an agentic TPU kernel generator combining LLM reasoning with real-time compiler feedback. Interactive, fully automated and graph-search modes clear 50 out of 50 JAXBench workloads for compilation and correctness, match or beat hand-tuned baselines on 7 of 8 real-world workloads, and produce a 7.85× speedup on DeepSeek V4 Sparse Attention prefill.

MaxKernel is Google's answer to the CUDA-versus-TPU kernel gap. TPUs have long lagged Nvidia GPUs on programmer ergonomics for custom kernel work because Pallas and the TPU compiler stack are less well-populated with hand-tuned reference libraries than CUDA. The Nine-author DeepMind and Google Research team, led by Nina Cai and Charles Hong, built a system that treats kernel writing as an agentic loop: an LLM authors a Pallas-JAX candidate, a fast pipeline compiles and benchmarks it against XLA reference code, the failure or perf-gap trace flows back to the LLM, and the loop repeats until a target speedup is met or the agent gives up.

The evaluation is unusually rigorous for an agentic-kernel paper. On JAXBench, a suite the authors assembled from Google-internal production workloads, MaxKernel achieves a 1.58× geometric mean speedup and a 50-of-50 correctness rate. On a set of eight real-world model workloads including MoE routing, sparse attention, and mixed-precision GEMM variants, MaxKernel matches or beats hand-tuned baselines on 7 of 8. The headline number is a 7.85× speedup on DeepSeek V4's Sparse Attention prefill operation, which for a MoE model of DeepSeek V4's shape translates to a roughly 30 percent reduction in end-to-end prefill latency on Trillium TPUs. A second production number: the paper reports a 2.32× geometric mean speedup over reference code across a broader benchmark of research-model kernels.

"Real-time compiler feedback is what makes an agentic kernel-writing loop work. Without it, the LLM proposes plausible code that does not compile." — From the paper's method section

The three-mode design is the practical detail worth reading. Interactive mode is a human-in-the-loop where a kernel author converses with MaxKernel to accelerate development. Fully automated mode gives MaxKernel a target and budget and returns the best kernel it can find. Graph-based autonomous search is the most novel: MaxKernel branches from a candidate to explore a search tree of variants under a compute budget, similar to a monte carlo tree search over algorithm choice. The reference implementation is open on the AI-Hypercomputer/accelerator-agents GitHub repo. The paper is expected to accelerate the timeline for open-source TPU kernel work Google's Cloud team has been publishing gradually since Trillium launch.

Bilevel Coordinated Reflection paper gives multi-agent LLMs a game-theoretic frame, lifts SWE-Bench to 72.2

arXiv 2609.02750 / Hugging Face Papers · 2026-09-07 · Source ↗

Yuxiang Chen, Yuxuan Huang, Meng Fang, Weilin Luo, and Jun Wang modeled orchestrator-worker interactions as a bilevel coordination game and proved workers' local update game is an approximate potential game whose equilibrium slack is bounded by decomposition quality. Their SRMA algorithm hits 72.2 percent on a 500-instance SWE-bench slice versus a 70.8 percent baseline.

The Bilevel Coordinated Reflection paper is the highest-upvoted paper on Sunday's Hugging Face Papers of the Day at 94 upvotes. Its contribution is a game-theoretic analysis of the orchestrator-worker structure that dominates production multi-agent LLM systems: one orchestrator model routes subtasks to a pool of worker models, and both the orchestrator and the workers reflect on their own outputs to improve them. The theoretical claim: the workers' local update game is an approximate potential game whose equilibrium slack is bounded by η_c ≤ 2d_max · κ, where κ is a decomposition-quality parameter. Higher decomposition quality by the orchestrator translates directly to tighter worker equilibrium slack, formalizing the intuition that a good orchestrator matters more than a good worker.

The impossibility result is the piece practitioners will cite most. The authors prove no gate that observes only the generated transcript can uniformly improve worker performance over text-indistinguishable environments. Practical translation: you cannot build a reflection loop that reads only what the model said and reliably improves it. You need environment-grounded verification, which is why every production agent stack that works well runs test suites, database queries, or browser assertions between reflection turns. The authors propose Stochastic Reflective Memory Ascent (SRMA), an algorithm that uses verifier-gated acceptance to guarantee geometric or polynomial convergence rates. Empirically on 500 SWE-bench instances, the full SRMA system resolves 72.2 percent versus a 70.8 percent baseline, a 1.4-point lift.

"Free-form reflection exhibits bounded error floors. Convergence requires environment-grounded verification, not text-only gates." — From the paper's abstract

The code ships at github.com/YihangChen9/Bilevel-Coordinated-Reflection under an Apache 2.0 license and is small enough to be worth reading in an afternoon. The paper's likely downstream impact is theoretical rather than product: it gives the framework community a clean formalization to build on for orchestrator-quality metrics, and it names a specific pitfall (text-only reflection gates) that a lot of production code still contains.

AllSpark Iris paper posts a search-agent scaling curve worth watching

arXiv 2609.04304 / Hugging Face Papers · 2026-09-07 · Source ↗

The second-most-upvoted Sunday paper at 50 upvotes and 3 comments describes AllSpark Research's Iris system, a search agent that treats retrieval as a search-frontier expansion problem. The paper introduces a scaling curve tying agent performance to the size of the retrieval index and the depth of the reasoning trace.

Iris is the second-highest-upvoted Sunday paper on Hugging Face's Daily Papers board and describes an approach to search agents that reframes retrieval as a search-frontier expansion. Rather than the standard retrieve-then-reason pipeline where a fixed number of documents flow into a fixed context, Iris treats the retrieval pool as a graph whose frontier the agent expands one candidate at a time based on which retrieved document most reduces uncertainty about the answer. The reasoning trace lengthens the frontier expansion adaptively: harder questions expand the frontier further before the agent commits.

The paper's headline contribution is a scaling curve. Iris performance rises log-linearly with two parameters: the size of the retrieval index and the maximum expansion depth. The curve holds across HotpotQA, TriviaQA, MuSiQue and the paper's proprietary AllSpark Search Frontier benchmark, and it does not flatten within the range the paper explores. That is the piece that pulled the upvotes; a clean scaling story is what practitioners are looking for from a search-agent paper right now. The paper is expected to inform AllSpark's forthcoming enterprise search product and is understood to have been vetted by Google Research and Perplexity's engineering team before publication.

Peking University's Motion-Omni jointly generates speech and full-body motion in one model

arXiv 2609.04250 / Hugging Face Papers · 2026-09-07 · Source ↗

The Peking University team behind Motion-Omni put an end-to-end joint speech and full-body motion generator on arXiv Sunday. The model produces speech waveforms and 3D pose trajectories from a single dialogue transcript, replacing the two-stage speech-then-animation pipelines dominant in embodied character AI.

Motion-Omni is Peking University's response to the two-stage bottleneck in embodied conversational agent research. Current speech-motion systems separate text-to-speech from motion synthesis, producing a speech waveform and then animating a body on top of it. The two-stage pipeline visibly lags on natural gesture-speech alignment, particularly on emphasis timing and gesture-word coupling. Motion-Omni is a single decoder that emits interleaved speech-audio and 3D pose tokens on a shared timeline, letting the model learn gesture-speech coupling in an end-to-end fashion rather than through a downstream alignment.

The paper posted 35 upvotes and one comment on Hugging Face's Sunday feed. The model is trained on a dataset the authors curated from actor-captured dialogue with synchronized motion capture, and the evaluation shows a 34 percent reduction in perceived speech-gesture mistiming on human-rater blind trials versus a Meta AudioMotion baseline. The likely downstream application is game-engine and VTuber character animation, where two-stage pipelines have persistent quality issues on emphasis moments the joint model is meant to fix.

WorldSculpt paper generates compositional 3D worlds from grounded video

arXiv 2609.05416 / Hugging Face Papers · 2026-09-07 · Source ↗

WorldSculpt takes a set of grounded videos and generates compositional 3D worlds a user can then edit object-by-object. The paper on Sunday's Hugging Face board posted 32 upvotes, and the method treats each video as evidence for a stack of asset primitives rather than as a single reconstruction target.

WorldSculpt takes an angle that is meaningfully different from the standard NeRF-and-diffusion 3D-reconstruction stack. Instead of treating each video as a single reconstruction target that outputs a single mesh, WorldSculpt decomposes each video into a stack of asset primitives, each with its own semantic label and pose. The output is a compositional 3D scene where a user can edit the tree, delete it, or move it independently of the ground plane, in the way a game-engine artist would.

The evaluation is qualitative more than quantitative. The paper compares WorldSculpt to Meta's SceneScript and Nvidia's WorldGen 3 on a set of 40 grounded video inputs and reports that human raters preferred WorldSculpt outputs on editability, semantic labeling accuracy, and asset separation. The trade-off is on holistic-reconstruction fidelity, where the compositional decomposition sometimes miscarves boundaries between adjacent objects. The paper's contribution is that it opens a compositional path other reconstruction methods can borrow from, and it is likely to influence game-engine content pipelines where editability matters more than photometric fidelity.

Princeton UniMate paper animates diverse skeletons with a single model

arXiv 2609.05415 / Hugging Face Papers · 2026-09-07 · Source ↗

Princeton's UniMate paper is a single motion-generation model that animates diverse skeletal topologies, from human bipeds to quadrupeds to invented characters, without retraining. The paper posted 12 upvotes and 49 comments on Sunday, with the comment volume driven by asset-pipeline artists asking about integration.

UniMate's contribution is topology-agnostic motion generation. Standard motion-generation models are trained on a specific skeletal topology, usually the SMPL human body, and cannot generate motion for a quadruped or a fictional creature without retraining or per-topology adaptation. UniMate encodes the skeletal topology as an explicit graph input to the model, letting a single trained model animate humans, dogs, dragons, or user-invented rigs without retraining. The paper reports comparable quality to per-topology baselines on human motion, dramatically better than any cross-topology baseline on quadruped motion, and above-baseline quality on user-invented topologies the model has never seen.

The Sunday Hugging Face comment thread ran to 49 comments largely from Blender and Unity artists asking about rig-file format support and inference latency on consumer hardware. The paper's authors responded that inference runs at roughly 4 fps on an RTX 4090, which is slower than production animation pipelines require. A follow-up paper is understood to be in preparation focused on latency optimization for real-time game-engine use.

The paper's contribution is not the interface, which is one of dozens shipped this year; the contribution is the argument that AI-scientist workflows should be explicitly designed around what the authors call "vibe research," a practice of running many small experiments in parallel and following the results by intuition rather than by pre-registered hypothesis. The paper accepts EMNLP 2026 Systems Demonstrations, and the release is timed against that venue.

The 1,060-comment volume on Sunday's Hugging Face board is the story. The comments split between researchers who see vibe-research as a legitimate methodology that mirrors how effective ML research actually happens, and researchers who argue the methodology is a rebranding of untracked ad-hoc experimentation and undermines reproducibility. The dispute connects to a broader industry conversation about what an automated AI researcher agent should be optimizing: pre-registered hypothesis validation, or vibe-research-style exploration. The paper's real impact is putting a rigorous vocabulary on that split.

"They see our technology as a true ally. It helps them find suitable tenders, understand requirements, and prepare bids, increasing winning chances." — Andrea Zorzetto, Cato CEO and co-founder
