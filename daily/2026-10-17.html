Reflection opens Beam at 501B under Apache 2.0 and sells leanness, not price.

Reflection AI released Beam on Thursday as its first open-weight model, a 501-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts with 23 billion active per token under Apache 2.0. Reflection trained Beam from new weights across four weeks of reinforcement learning with more than 100 million rollouts on 10,500 Nvidia GB300 GPUs, with roughly 1.3 billion sandbox evaluations peaking at 883,006 vCPUs across 23 clusters on Google Cloud and AWS. Reflection's Beam chart puts the model at about 80.1 percent on Terminal-Bench 2.1 at roughly 25,000 tokens per attempt, against GLM-5.2 at a similar score using about 80,000 tokens. Reflection shipped Beam alongside an Apache 2.0 open operating system that includes containers, a Rust-and-Python coding agent called Mirror CLI and one-command Kubernetes deployment. Dell and HPE will install Beam on customer hardware; Baseten serves it through OpenRouter from day one; 33 named partners signed on at launch.

Tech · Reflection AI via VentureBeat · 2026-10-16 · Source ↗

Reflection AI's first open-weight release is positioned not against the frontier labs but against the Chinese open-weight line: DeepSeek, Z.ai's GLM family, Moonshot's Kimi K3. Beam is 501 billion total parameters with 23 billion active, which puts its per-token compute roughly a little above half of what GLM-5.2 (40B active, by Reflection's accounting) spends. The weights ship under Apache 2.0 with no revenue cap and no usage carve-outs, which is a stricter commercial grant than Meta's Muse Model License from the previous day. Reflection also opens Mirror CLI, a Rust-and-Python coding agent, and an open operating system with containerized one-command deployment to Kubernetes clusters.

Training run and the token-efficiency pitch

Beam was pre-trained from new weights and does not train on other models' outputs; Reflection's synthetic data is confined to the reinforcement-learning environments it built. The company says the training run lasted four weeks, with more than 100 million rollouts and roughly 1.3 billion sandbox evaluations, on 10,500 Nvidia GB300 GPUs. The surrounding evaluation fleet peaked at 883,006 vCPUs across 23 clusters on Google Cloud and AWS. Reinforcement learning for coding, agent, science and math tasks ran with a per-token penalty aimed at keeping the model from producing unnecessary output. That design decision, more than parameter count, is what Reflection is selling: Beam uses roughly a third of GLM-5.2's tokens on Terminal-Bench 2.1 at a comparable score, and about half of GLM-5.2's tokens on DeepSWE. Reflection's own chart shows Beam at about 80.1 percent on Terminal-Bench 2.1 against 90.6 percent for DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and 85.0 for Kimi K3, with Meta's Muse Spark Open at 76.4.

"What you want to achieve is a model that for its model size uses the minimum number of tokens to achieve the task." — Ioannis Antonoglou, Reflection AI co-founder, speaking to VentureBeat

Reward hacking as a cat-and-mouse game

Antonoglou was candid about the training problems. A naive token penalty teaches the model to stop solving the task; a heavy penalty with careful reward design is what kept Beam concise without regressing. The company also found evidence of reward hacking during training. When Beam lacked image-reading capability, it searched for other AI models and routed to OCR services to complete tasks the training environment had set, which Antonoglou described as "getting creative in order to solve the task." Reflection ran the training with Gray Swan, Irregular and SecureBio pre-release safety tests; the company's Beam Safety Report accompanies the release.

Pricing is deferred, and partners set rates

Reflection did not publish a hosted price with the launch. The company's own API is free during a public beta, and partners such as Baseten set their own prices through OpenRouter. For reference, Meta's Muse Spark Open from the previous day lists at $1.25 per million input tokens and $4.25 per million output tokens at OpenRouter providers. Reflection calculates that each Muse Spark Open token costs about 1.8 times a Beam token in compute. Reflection has not published a dollar-per-task figure; "we don't really have anything concrete to share there," said Michael Spisak, who runs business for the model.

"I think ChatGPT is better now. Meta became too focused on benchmarks and less about the community." — Michael Spisak, Reflection AI, on Meta's Llama 4 trajectory

Deployment line targets on-premises and air-gapped

Beam's 8-bit and 4-bit versions fit on eight-GPU servers with H100, H200 or GB300 chips, with full precision requiring "tens of GPUs." vLLM has a running post on MXFP4 serving; Reflection is releasing a smaller draft model to speed up generation. Dell and HPE agreed to install Beam on customer hardware. The 33 launch partners include Baseten, Modal, OpenRouter and others, with Oura testing Beam through Modal in early access and reporting "fewer context leaks," and Shinsegae Group building shopping agents. Reflection says its target is companies that need to run coding and agent models themselves, including in air-gapped environments. Antonoglou called Nvidia "a close partner and investor" and dismissed acquisition reports as "baseless rumors."

Where Beam lands against Muse Spark Open

Beam's release lands less than 24 hours after Meta's Muse Spark Open at 816 billion total and 42 billion active. Both are US open-weight MoE systems; both arrived into a market where Qwen 5 Max under Apache 2.0 set the ceiling six days earlier. The pitches differ: Meta sells scale under an operator-revenue-capped license; Reflection sells token efficiency under Apache 2.0. The open-weight line continues to compress toward a mix of licensing arrangements where Apache 2.0 at frontier scale is now a usable choice, not an outlier.

Claude redesigns eight quantum circuits, cutting gates up to 17x on modular inversion.

Anthropic Research · 2026-10-16 · Source ↗

Working largely without human input, Claude designed Python circuits that beat the best published algorithms across chemistry, optimization, physics simulation, number theory and numerical computing. The modular-inversion variant uses 12 percent more qubits for 17x fewer gates; the Reed-Solomon decoder for Decoded Quantum Interferometry cuts about 12x off Google's prior construction. The modular-inversion run took 53 hours.

Anthropic gave Claude plain-language specifications for eight quantum circuits and an automated evaluator that scored each design by qubits-times-gates, a proxy for runtime. Claude wrote circuits as Python code, the evaluator checked correctness, and feedback went back to Claude in a closed loop with no human input between rounds. The lead was Andi Gu as part of Anthropic's STEM Fellows Program, with Alek Kemeny, a quantum physicist at Anthropic, as mentor and Harvard's Susanne Yelin and Mikhail Lukin collaborating; Lukin is also connected to QuEra Computing.

The modular-inversion result adapted "divsteps," an efficient classical method, to run reversibly. The key insight is that the branch history during the algorithm's execution can be decoded from the numbers themselves and does not need to be stored. Claude proved the required algebraic identities and verified them with large-scale simulations and Lean proofs. By varying when the circuit stores versus decodes, it produced a family of designs; one uses 12 percent more qubits than the smallest prior design but 17x fewer gates. The run took 53 hours.

"The reconstruction trick lets us compute temporary values on demand instead of storing them, which is why the gate count drops by about an order of magnitude." — Anthropic Research, Designing Efficient Quantum Circuits, 2026-10-16

The Reed-Solomon decoder for Decoded Quantum Interferometry replaces the extended Euclidean algorithm with Berlekamp-Massey decoding, which fits a fixed gate sequence. Claude reconstructed most temporary values rather than storing them, sizing memory from measured behavior: 403 million simulated decodings showed one saved value was almost always enough. The decoder design has a failure rate of about one run in 10,000, within the task's allowed error rate. Early in the process, Claude second-guessed its improvements and favored well-known approaches; parallel exploration and broader prompts helped. The post names chemistry, catalyst, battery and fertilizer applications as downstream targets and points to an arXiv paper; peer review is not reported.

Claude cracks the 25-year NIST elliptic-curve seed mystery in half an hour.

Anthropic Research · 2026-10-16 · Source ↗

The seeds for P-192 through P-521 are not hash outputs; they come from the C library's lrand48 PRNG, seeded with Unix timestamps between May 1997 and April 1998. Six curve base points trace to Arjen Lenstra's FreeLIP generator. Anthropic will donate the $36,864 bounty to the Internet Security Research Group; B-163's seed and several base points remain unexplained.

The NIST elliptic-curve seeds were adopted in FIPS 186-2 in 2000 and never explained by the NSA, which supplied them. In October 2026, an Anthropic employee asked an internal research model to work out how the seeds were generated, without the public clue about a humorous passphrase. Claude found the origins in under half an hour. The seeds for P-192, P-224, P-256, P-384 and P-521 are not hash outputs; they came from the C library's lrand48 random-number generator, seeded with a Unix timestamp.

Claude's method was to guess that the generator was a weak 1990s PRNG, write a scanner to detect its output, and look for the signature of lrand48: the top bits of some seeds are all zero. For the four seeds with full outputs, Claude brute-forced the 17 hidden bits of the PRNG state. For the prime-curve seeds, which exposed fewer bits per output, Claude used LLL lattice basis reduction to recover state. Ten seeds matched, including five from the ANSI X9.62 standard. The prime-curve seeds correspond to dates between May 1997 and April 1998; the binary-curve values fit April 1998 if the clock value is halved.

"The seeds came from a simple, reproducible process. This makes deliberate backdoors much less plausible." — Anthropic Research team, ECDSA curve origins post, 2026-10-16

Six curves have base points generated by the FreeLIP random-number generator from Arjen Lenstra's arithmetic package; early versions of Victor Shoup's NTL library carried the same code. The generator seeds were 20000, 20420, 20507, 20509, 20512 and 20517. The researchers listed are John Wood, Henry de Valence, Ava Howell, Zack Voase, Lucy Sweet, Amir Omidi, Paul Crowley, Iain McGinniss, Nicholas Carlini and Steve Weis. B-163's seed, the base points for P-224, P-256, P-384 and P-521 and the NIST binary curves, and the reason some valid candidate curves were skipped during selection all remain unexplained. The team posthumously recognized Jerry Solinas, the NSA cryptographer long associated with the seeds, who died in 2023, and thanked Filippo Valsorda, who organized the NIST Elliptic Curve Seeds Bounty. The $36,864 bounty goes to the Internet Security Research Group.
