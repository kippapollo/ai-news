OpenAI says an unreleased model cracked Navier-Stokes with 10,000 agents. NYU's Buckmaster says they read his Codex notes.

OpenAI announced Tuesday that an unreleased frontier model, coordinating roughly 10,000 autonomous agents over 88 hours and exchanging almost 5 million messages, produced a Lean-verified singularity proof for the 3D Navier-Stokes equations, one of the Clay Institute's seven Millennium Prize Problems. Twelve hours before the OpenAI post went live, NYU's Tristan Buckmaster and Anthropic's Levent Alpöge released a Lean-verified 3D Euler blowup proof and accused OpenAI's team of pursuing the same result after seeing their private Codex work. Sébastien Bubeck estimated OpenAI's compute bill at several million dollars. OpenAI conceded 3D Euler priority to Buckmaster and Alpöge but claimed Navier-Stokes for its own model. Charles Fefferman of Princeton, who wrote the Clay Institute's official Navier-Stokes problem description, credited Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa as "the heroes of the story" and said their work sits underneath everything else.

Tech · Safety · Quanta Magazine / OpenAI / Fortune · 2026-09-08 · Source ↗

OpenAI's post appeared Tuesday afternoon in San Francisco and made the specific claim that Nearly 100 agents worked together for approximately 50 hours to produce our Euler regularity disproof and that 10,000 or so agents worked for 88 hours to produce the Navier-Stokes existence-and-smoothness proof, exchanging almost 5 million messages to each other. Sébastien Bubeck, the OpenAI researcher who led the effort, estimated the compute cost in the several-million-dollar range. The model that produced the proof is unreleased. OpenAI has not published the Lean file or the intermediate reasoning transcripts. Quanta Magazine's Erica Klarreich, who has been reporting on the effort, published the definitive Tuesday walk-through and framed the result as a claim rather than a settled proof: Lean-verified means the proof compiles, but a human still has to verify that what Lean is proving corresponds to the original Millennium Prize statement.

Twelve hours before the OpenAI post, Tristan Buckmaster of NYU Courant and Levent Alpöge of Anthropic released a Lean-verified proof of finite-time blowup for three fluid systems including 3D incompressible Euler, following a body of work by Diego Córdoba of ICMAT and Luis Martínez-Zoroa (currently at ETH Zürich) that has been building over the past three years. Buckmaster's public statement is that his private Codex sessions containing scratch-work and intermediate lemmas may have been accessible to OpenAI's research team, and that OpenAI began an intense push on the fluid-dynamics problems in the two weeks after his team's approach was shared with a Codex-adjacent collaborator. Buckmaster's exact framing on X read that the timing "is suspicious enough that I would want an audit." OpenAI's response was categorical: researchers and agents did not see any of their work through any means until they released it publicly, and no specific user data was accessed in order to solve this problem.

"The first LLM-generated proof Levent sent me was the most horrendous I have ever read. But there was a real result inside." — Tristan Buckmaster, NYU Courant, on the Buckmaster-Alpöge Euler blowup proof

What each team proved

Buckmaster and Alpöge's release covers Lean-verified proofs of finite-time singularity formation for three fluid PDE systems, most importantly the 3D incompressible Euler equations. Their work extends Córdoba and Martínez-Zoroa's earlier constructions with the addition of formal verification, and it is now sitting on Lean Zulip for line-by-line audit. OpenAI ceded 3D Euler priority to Buckmaster and Alpöge in its post. OpenAI's own claim is the harder one: existence-and-smoothness for the 3D incompressible Navier-Stokes equations, the exact statement the Clay Mathematics Institute posted a $1 million prize for in 2000. If the OpenAI proof holds under expert review, Bubeck and his team would have solved one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems using an autonomous agent swarm on a model OpenAI has not released, and the effort would be the first Millennium Prize claim made entirely by an AI system. Charles Fefferman, the Princeton mathematician who wrote the Clay Institute's official Navier-Stokes problem description, told Quanta he was thrilled the problem was solved and named Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa as the underlying architects of the approach.

"Luis Martínez-Zoroa deserves a Fields Medal for this. He is the person who saw the way through." — Tristan Buckmaster, NYU, on the underlying construction

The Codex-access dispute

The Codex-access allegation is the piece of the story that will drive the next month of coverage. Buckmaster's account, given to Fortune's Sharon Goldman, is that he and Alpöge used private Codex sessions to draft, edit, and iterate on their proof between June and August. In late August he shared a summary of the approach with a Codex user who is also affiliated with OpenAI's research team. Two weeks later OpenAI's agent swarm produced its own proof on a closely related problem. OpenAI's response is that no specific user data was accessed, and that Codex-side telemetry can prove it. The company said it will publish an audit log demonstrating no researcher on the Navier-Stokes team had access to Buckmaster's Codex sessions. Simon Willison covered the dispute Tuesday evening on his blog and flagged the deeper structural question the incident raises: if a Codex-adjacent OpenAI researcher happened to be shown Buckmaster's approach through legitimate channels rather than a data-leak, would OpenAI still owe a citation and priority acknowledgment?

What Lean verification does and doesn't guarantee

Lean is a theorem prover developed at Microsoft Research whose type checker will only accept a proof term when every step type-checks against the axioms of dependent type theory. A Lean-verified proof is airtight in the sense that the derivation compiles. It does not guarantee that what the proof establishes is what a mathematician thought they were establishing. In the Navier-Stokes case the load-bearing translation step is the Lean formalization of the Millennium Prize statement itself. If OpenAI's Lean formalization diverges from Clay's precise statement of the smoothness condition, or if it introduces auxiliary axioms that mathematicians consider non-standard, the "proof" would need to be re-scrutinized. Buckmaster and Alpöge's Euler proof has already gone through a public Lean-file audit on the Lean Zulip and has held up. OpenAI has committed to publishing its Lean file within days, and the mathematical community's audit will begin as soon as the file is public.

Where this leaves the field

The result, if it holds, is the second Millennium Prize claim in a year (the Poincaré Conjecture was already solved by Grigori Perelman in 2003, and Fermat's Last Theorem was proved by Wiles in the 1990s outside the Millennium framework). It is the first Millennium claim generated by an AI system rather than a human. It is also the second machine-checkable AI-assisted proof of a major open problem this quarter, following Anthropic's Prove2Me-based Lean proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in July. What the Buckmaster dispute has already established, regardless of the audit outcome, is that the norms for authorship, data provenance, and credit-sharing in AI-assisted mathematics need to catch up with the technology by the end of 2026. Charles Fefferman closed the Quanta piece with a line worth quoting in full: "I don't yet know if these results will be verified. If they are, it will be a triumph. If they are not, it will be an important cautionary tale."

DeepMind ships AlphaGenome Atlas: 1 petabyte of precomputed predictions across all 9B human DNA variants

Google DeepMind / Unite.AI / IEEE Spectrum · 2026-09-08 · Source ↗

DeepMind precomputed predictions from its AlphaGenome sequence-to-function model for every possible single-letter variant of the human genome, producing a 1-petabyte dataset roughly 30 times the AlphaFold Database. A new AVI score summarizes coding-and-non-coding regulatory impact for each position, and the atlas is open to non-commercial research via a website, an API and a Google Antigravity skill.

AlphaGenome is DeepMind's foundation model for reading DNA sequence context and predicting what a given nucleotide substitution does to downstream molecular readouts (gene expression, splicing, chromatin state). The Atlas is what you get when you run that model exhaustively rather than one variant at a time: predictions across all 9 billion possible single-letter changes in the human genome, packaged as a queryable 1-petabyte dataset. The scale is roughly 30x the AlphaFold Database at its 2022 launch, which itself changed how structural biology gets done. Each variant gets an AVI (AlphaGenome Variant Impact) score summarizing predicted molecular consequence across both coding and non-coding regions, giving clinical researchers a single number to filter and rank rare-disease candidate variants before pursuing wet-lab follow-up.

"AlphaGenome Atlas is a research tool. It predicts molecular effects and can serve only as part of the evidence chain leading to clinical diagnoses, not as sufficient evidence on their own." — DeepMind's Sept 8 paper disclaimer

The distribution plan matters: the atlas is free for non-commercial research use, available through a queryable API, and exposed as a first-class skill inside Google Antigravity, DeepMind's agentic development platform. That last piece is the pipeline: a rare-disease researcher can ask an Antigravity agent to rank all missense variants in a candidate gene by AVI score, cross-reference against ClinVar, pull matched-tissue expression evidence, and produce a ranked short-list without leaving the agent conversation. Regulatory readers will note DeepMind's careful language that AlphaGenome has not been validated or approved for any clinical use; the Atlas is positioned as evidence for a diagnostic hypothesis, not a diagnostic itself. IEEE Spectrum's coverage was the most technically careful and noted the two open questions the biology community will pursue: how does AVI perform on variants underrepresented in training data, and how do predictions calibrate across ancestry groups that differ in reference-genome coverage.

IFM's Uno paper delivers 3× lossless speedups on 8B autoregressive LLMs via discrete diffusion

arXiv 2609.04010 / Hugging Face Papers · 2026-09-08 · Source ↗

Subham Sekhar Sahoo and 16 co-authors from the Institute of Foundation Models introduce Uno, a family of diffusion-augmented LLMs that draw multiple tokens per step in parallel from an existing AR model. The 8B Uno variant outperforms DiffusionGemma-26B and proprietary Mercury 2 on agentic tool use, coding and long-context reasoning at up to 3× the base AR model throughput.

Uno's contribution is a mechanism the authors call Ψ-Spec: a discrete-diffusion sampler that generates multiple tokens per forward pass while remaining provably lossless against the base autoregressive distribution. The model architecture separates parameters into "AR weights" (the frozen base model) and "diffusion weights" (a small trainable adapter), and the diffusion weights are trained via a technique called Diffusion Distillation on trajectories from the base AR model. The result: no separate draft model is required (as it would be for speculative decoding), and the output distribution exactly matches what the AR model would have produced, no quality loss for the throughput gain.

"Uno-8B outperforms the leading open diffusion LLM DiffusionGemma and the proprietary Mercury 2 across agentic tool use, coding, and long-context reasoning." — From the paper's abstract

The benchmark set is the practical piece. Uno-8B beats DiffusionGemma-26B (three times larger) and Inception Labs' Mercury 2 across tool use, coding and long-context reasoning, at up to 3x the base AR-model throughput at every evaluated batch size. The paper posted at s-sahoo.github.io/uno/ with code and checkpoints and reached 50 upvotes on the Hugging Face Papers-of-the-Day board on Tuesday. The technique lands at exactly the moment the diffusion-LLM story is heating up — Inception Labs shipped Mercury 2.5 the same day at 1,107 tokens per second — and gives the field a cleaner comparison target: a lossless diffusion adapter that can be attached to any AR model rather than a from-scratch diffusion LLM. IFM was founded earlier in 2026 by ex-Google Research and ex-Cerebras researchers.

Tencent Hunyuan's FlowBalance paper introduces verifier-grounded self-improvement without token-level imitation loss

arXiv 2609.03241 · 2026-09-08 · Source ↗

Zixun Huang, Kishan Panaganti, Haitao Mi and Leowei Liang propose FlowBalance: a verifier-grounded self-improvement method that calibrates trajectory-level self-guidance scores with verifier-derived group advantages, achieving outcome-calibrated policy updates without a separate token-level imitation loss. The 28-page paper posted on Sept 8 and included 7 figures and 10 tables.

FlowBalance sits in the class of self-improvement algorithms that let a policy learn from its own on-policy reasoning trajectories under verifier supervision — the same problem space as OpenAI's o1 self-play and Anthropic's constitutional-AI-derived RLHF variants. The technical contribution: for each on-policy trajectory, a "frozen training-time view of the same policy" uses privileged context to produce token-level log-probability gains, which are aggregated into a trajectory-level self-guidance score. FlowBalance calibrates this score against a verifier-derived group advantage, and delivers outcome-calibrated self-guidance via trajectory balance — no separate token-level imitation loss is required.

The practical benefit: token-level imitation losses are expensive to compute and can distort exploration by penalizing legitimate policy divergences from a teacher. FlowBalance's trajectory-balance formulation avoids that penalty while still using verifier feedback to calibrate the improvement signal. The paper reports gains on standard mathematical-reasoning benchmarks; the interesting piece for practitioners is that the method is a drop-in replacement for RLHF-style verifier training on any base model. Tencent Hunyuan's frontier program is on the same technical trajectory as OpenAI's o-series and Anthropic's reasoning stack, and the FlowBalance publication makes a concrete design choice public that Western labs can respond to.
