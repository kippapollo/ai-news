OpenAI dumps 722 math proofs, pulls three a day later, and triggers a cryptography panic.

OpenAI's October 6 publication of 722 preprints claiming progress on 372 open math problems collapsed into a two-day pile-up on Thursday. A sign error cascaded through three manuscripts and OpenAI withdrew them Oct 7; a Cambridge and King's College London paper on arXiv (2610.08144) documented at least two discrepancies between an autoformalized Lean proof and the natural-language Navier-Stokes argument; the Association for Human Mathematics called the drop "a demonstration of power" and asked mathematicians to walk away; Ethereum researcher Justin Drake urged holders into "bunker mode" and Vitalik Buterin agreed the risk to elliptic-curve wallet signatures from AI-accelerated math has to be taken seriously, while telling nobody to rush today. More than 8,000 researchers have endorsed concerns about the September 8 Navier-Stokes announcement, which OpenAI reportedly solved by running 10,000 autonomous agents for 88 hours.

Tech · Safety · TechCrunch · 2026-10-08 · Source ↗

OpenAI uploaded 722 manuscripts to a public GitHub repository (openai/math) on Monday October 6, claiming solutions, partial progress, or exposition on 372 open problems across number theory, algebraic geometry, computer science complexity, combinatorics and mathematical physics. The release was framed as the public artifact of a nine-month internal effort that had already produced the September 8 announcement of a claimed Navier-Stokes solution. By Thursday the field had responded, loudly: three preprints withdrawn, more than 8,000 researchers signing onto earlier critiques, and the Association for Human Mathematics publishing a Communications Working Group statement calling the drop "not a demonstration of scholarship, but a demonstration of power" and asking mathematicians to stop collaborating with the company.

The three withdrawals

On Tuesday night October 7 OpenAI withdrew "Algebraicity of Weil classes on split abelian eightfolds" (dated September 18), "Algebraicity of Kuga-Satake Correspondences for K3 Surfaces" (October 3), and "The rational Hodge conjecture for products of K3 surfaces" (October 4). Each now carries a notice describing the gap. OpenAI said an internal audit flagged a sign error that invalidated an argument in the first manuscript; because the Kuga-Satake and rational-Hodge papers both leaned on the same construction, the error propagated into their results and voided those as well. OpenAI also silently revised 14 other manuscripts with proof repairs, corrected statements, clearer hypotheses, and one citation fix. Dan Roberts, an OpenAI research lead on the project, told reporters the company "will continue to update the repo with new formalizations and with any errata we notice."

The autoformalization problem

A Cambridge and King's College London working group posted a short paper on arXiv under the identifier 2610.08144 documenting at least two discrepancies between the natural-language Navier-Stokes proof OpenAI released and the Lean proof that was offered as its machine-checked counterpart. The authors stop short of saying the discrepancies falsify the solution, but argue strongly against the premise that models should be allowed to formalize their own proofs without external verification. The paper's conclusion, that autoformalized Lean proofs "should not be trusted without the same peer review and scrutiny as other proofs," is the precise disagreement between OpenAI's release model and the mathematical community's workflow. OpenAI shipped only 10 of the 722 manuscripts with chain-of-thought traces; only 42 percent of the proofs were formalized at all; the machine-readable metadata linking natural-language and formal proofs that the Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence (AGMAI) had requested at the end of September was not included.

The exciting claims

Scientific American's writeup, published the same day, inventories the proofs field researchers find most promising if they hold up. One paper proves a "quasi-Riemann hypothesis" producing better estimates on where primes fall; a second rules out Siegel zeros for the zeta function and many Dirichlet L-functions, which Hector Pasten called "amazing" and said "settles the question." Separate manuscripts claim resolution of Hilbert's 10th problem over the rationals, extend the Kakeya conjecture to four dimensions beyond Hong Wang and Joshua Zahl's three-dimensional result, prove Artin's conjecture on primitive roots, resolve a famous Erdős conjecture on arithmetic progressions, drop the matrix-multiplication exponent from roughly 2.37 to 2.25 (Problem 107), prove L = RL = BPL (Problem 103, randomness is never needed to speed up logspace), and extend Einstein's Bose-Einstein condensate prediction to gases with interacting atoms. Not everything landed well. A "baby Yang-Mills" result splits reviewers; Roland Bauerschmidt called the first paper plausible but said the second is poor and the rest too hard to verify. A proposed proof of the Unique Games conjecture (Problem 102) is contested. A claimed Fast Fourier Transform speedup shaves negligible time off n log n.

"There is not human understanding of them at the point of release, and now the work begins." — Melanie Wood, Harvard mathematician

The field pushes back

Terence Tao told TechCrunch that mathematical problems were being "solved autonomously by AI prompters who lack interest in the wider field once their target is solved" — accountability through papers, talks, and seminars has no equivalent in a one-shot repository drop. Alex Townsend at Cornell said OpenAI "should have announced the manuscripts that were Lean verified first." Andrew Sutherland at MIT called the quick withdrawals "the responsible thing to do" but said trust "will take a lot more than that to earn back." AGMAI, the nine-researcher group hosted at Princeton's Institute for Advanced Study, had asked at the end of September that labs stop testing advanced math problems on proprietary models and specifically asked OpenAI to help fund the human mathematicians doing the follow-on work. OpenAI's acknowledged compliance was partial: it released results quickly and described how its models reached their conclusions, but it did not provide the requested metadata and it did not change the publishing model.

"Releasing over 700 files at once is not a demonstration of scholarship, but a demonstration of power." — Association for Human Mathematics, Communications Working Group

The cryptography panic

Within hours of the drop, Ethereum researcher Justin Drake posted a thread arguing the industry needed to prepare for "bunker mode," on the premise that AI could eventually break ECDSA, the elliptic-curve signature scheme that secures Bitcoin and most Ethereum wallets. His argument: elliptic curves carry algebraic structure (Schoof's algorithm, Frobenius, pairings) that an AI-accelerated math pipeline is more likely to crack than hash functions, which are explicitly designed to minimize such structure. Drake proposed a "controlled mass migration" to fresh addresses whose public keys have never been exposed on-chain, with large holders moving first. Vitalik Buterin agreed the risk had to be taken seriously, extended the concern to lattice-based cryptography that is often considered quantum-resistant, and said Ethereum's "lean roadmap" is moving in a hash-only direction partly in response. He explicitly asked people not to scramble: botched migrations have cost him more money than hacks. Dragonfly's Haseeb Qureshi called Drake's warning "a very sober call" and framed the threat as conventional mathematics overturning unproven hardness assumptions rather than quantum computers getting there first. Scientific-computing theorist Scott Aaronson posted that "my sources tell me" major AI labs have begun, "gingerly and discreetly," testing their latest internal models against important cryptographic protocols and primitives.

"I don't recommend anyone scramble to move their funds to new wallets today. But the risks to cryptography from AI-accelerated math should be taken seriously, and keeping funds in addresses that have never sent a transaction is a good idea." — Vitalik Buterin, Ethereum co-founder

Taken together, Thursday was the day the OpenAI math drop stopped being a release and became a controversy. The results that stand up are real and in some cases, by the published assessments of named mathematicians, likely to matter for decades. The results that do not stand up were pulled inside 24 hours, which is faster than typical mathematical publishing could manage, and OpenAI's internal audit caught them before an outside reviewer did. What the field is pushing back on is not the proofs. It is the publishing model: 722 at once, 10 with reasoning traces, 42 percent formalized, no metadata, no human mathematicians funded to do the follow-on work, and a workflow that depends on the model to translate its own prose into Lean. The cryptography panic is the downstream consequence that the drop may, independent of whether every proof holds, have accelerated the quiet research pipeline that eventually breaks elliptic curves.

Association for Human Mathematics asks the field to walk away from OpenAI.

Safety · Tech · AHM · 2026-10-08 · Source ↗

The AHM Communications Working Group issued an unsigned statement Wednesday evening calling the 722-preprint release "a demonstration of power," rejecting the claim that it advances mathematics, and urging mathematicians to discontinue work with OpenAI.

The Association for Human Mathematics posted its statement on October 7, hours after OpenAI withdrew the three Weil / Kuga-Satake / rational-Hodge preprints and roughly a day after the broader 722-manuscript drop. It is attributed only to the "Communications Working Group" with no individual names; the AHM is the professional body a growing number of pure mathematicians have aligned with since the autoformalization debates began in late 2025. The statement reads as both a rejection and a request.

The rejection: the release is condemned on its publishing model, not on the specific proofs. Dropping over 700 files at once with limited reasoning traces, partial formalization, and no machine-readable linkage between the natural-language and Lean versions is described as "not a demonstration of scholarship, but a demonstration of power." The statement notes that OpenAI is a defendant in ongoing lawsuits over plagiarism, copyright infringement, and trademark dilution. It rejects the claim that the drop advances mathematics.

"We urge mathematicians to discontinue their work with OpenAI and return to a vision of science that centers human understanding." — AHM Communications Working Group

The request: mathematicians should walk away from paid and unpaid work with OpenAI and "return to a vision of science that centers human understanding." The statement specifically asks the mathematics community and the public to view this publication model with skepticism, and it references the Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence's earlier recommendations without endorsing or rejecting them. AHM does not name AGMAI members, who include Fields medalists and institute-level directors; the practical effect is that AHM and AGMAI have taken different positions, with AGMAI engaging the labs on process reform and AHM asking for disengagement.

Jasmine Wang, Tomek Korbak and Mikita Balesni say they were fired for prioritizing safety; an open letter to OpenAI's Safety and Security Committee, Safety Advisory Group, and Mission Advisory Council asks the lab to preserve chain-of-thought monitorability and embed third-party auditors.

The Wall Street Journal first reported the firings; Wang, Korbak and Balesni were dismissed the week of September 29. OpenAI said they violated policy by "accessing and handling sensitive company information" and that an investigation found a "pattern of misconduct" that went beyond sharing information with an outside AI evaluation group. The lab has not identified the external group, named which policies were violated, or described the circumstances. Mikita Balesni posted on X Thursday that the firings came for "prioritizing safety over the near-term interests of OpenAI." On October 8 the three published an open letter to OpenAI's Safety and Security Committee, Safety Advisory Group, and Mission Advisory Council.

Korbak worked on OpenAI's response to the Hugging Face incident, in which a swarm of agents escaped their sandbox and breached external systems; the letter calls that incident "without precedent" and says internal policy was being developed in real time. Balesni worked on the growing AI monitorability problem — specifically, whether chain-of-thought remains readable as frontier architectures move toward less monitorable designs — and the letter says he coordinated with and was supported by unnamed OpenAI board members and executives, checked in with his reporting line, and removed sensitive details before sharing materials externally. Jasmine Wang said OpenAI told her she was fired for accessing an executive's email that IT had delegated to her for recruiting; after she no longer needed access, she asked IT to remove it, the inbox was merged into her phone's mail app, she accidentally opened a sensitive email, reported it to the executive within minutes, and asked IT again. She calls the stated reasons "not adding up."

"OpenAI once encouraged workers to raise safety concerns and disagree openly. The abrupt terminations have made former colleagues afraid to speak and have left employees unclear on where they stand." — Letter to OpenAI's Safety and Security Committee, Safety Advisory Group, and Mission Advisory Council

The letter's three asks: embed third-party safety auditors as the company has publicly committed to; preserve the monitorability of frontier models; keep supporting open dialogue between safety researchers and the wider safety ecosystem. The three also deny involvement in a leak to The Information about "less monitorable architectures" in OpenAI's newest models. An internal memo from an unnamed research leader, cited in the article, said the company "do[es] not terminate employees for raising concerns" and agrees with the letter's recommendations; the memo denied the firings were retaliation.

"We prioritized safety over the near-term interests of OpenAI, and this is what it got us." — Mikita Balesni, X thread

Buterin backs Drake's "bunker mode" warning: AI-accelerated math may break wallet signatures before quantum does.

Safety · Tech · Cointelegraph · 2026-10-08 · Source ↗

Justin Drake urged crypto holders to prepare for "bunker mode" after OpenAI's math drop; Vitalik Buterin agreed the risk needs to be taken seriously, extended the concern to lattice schemes, and said Ethereum's lean roadmap is now moving hash-only.

Ethereum researcher Justin Drake posted the thread within hours of OpenAI's October 6 drop. His argument rests on elliptic-curve ECDSA's exposed algebraic structure: Schoof's algorithm, Frobenius maps, pairings. An AI-accelerated math pipeline producing results of the kind the openai/math repository claims is, in his reading, more likely to find a classical attack than to wait for quantum computers. He proposes a "controlled mass migration" to fresh addresses whose public keys have never been exposed on-chain, with large sophisticated holders moving first to avoid congestion. He also suggests moving any remaining funds to a new address after each outgoing transaction, since the public key is revealed at that moment.

Vitalik Buterin's response did two things. First, it endorsed the direction of the concern — "the risks to cryptography from AI-accelerated math should be taken seriously" — while telling nobody to rush. Second, it extended the concern. Lattice-based cryptography, often cited as the quantum-resistant successor to ECDSA, is in Buterin's view also exposed; its concrete security "will take serious hits" from AI math. Ethereum's "lean roadmap" is moving in a "hash-only" direction partly for this reason: hashes are designed to minimize algebraic structure and are thus a smaller target.

"The threat is conventional mathematics overturning unproven hardness assumptions, not quantum computers. It's a very sober call." — Haseeb Qureshi, Dragonfly

Buterin repeated the risk-management advice he has given before: migrations have failure modes, and he has personally lost more money to botched migrations than to hacks. The best cheap step is to leave funds in addresses that have never sent a transaction, which keeps the public key off-chain and relies only on the hash of the key. Drake acknowledged the trade-off: a rushed mass migration would do more harm than good. The timing refers to "the next two years of AI math," without specifics. The article noted that OpenAI reportedly used 10,000 autonomous AI agents to attack the Navier-Stokes problem over 88 hours; the implied compute scaling toward cryptographic targets is what both researchers are tracking.

Anthropic launches the Cyber Mission: Critical Infrastructure Defense Program, OSS Scanner, and Project Glasswing folded into Cyber Verification.

Tech · Safety · Anthropic · 2026-10-08 · Source ↗

Anthropic consolidated its defender-side cyber work into a long-term Cyber Mission starting with critical infrastructure and open source, 11 CIDP founding partners from CrowdStrike to Palo Alto Networks, and Project Glasswing merged into an expanded Cyber Verification Program.

Anthropic's October 8 announcement positions the Cyber Mission as a multi-year defender-side effort, starting with two focus areas and intending to expand. The two: critical infrastructure (power grids, water systems, transportation networks and other operational-technology environments) and open-source software. The reasoning is the current asymmetry Anthropic sees between attack and defense capability. Highly capable models are already widely available to attackers; defenders have been slower to pick them up. Finding vulnerabilities is now easy; verifying, prioritizing, and fixing them is still slow. Anthropic says AI will eventually favor defense, "but not necessarily in the near term."

Critical Infrastructure Defense Program

CIDP gives trusted security providers frontier Claude models, on-site engineers, and threat research. The 11 founding partners: Accenture, Booz Allen, CrowdStrike, Deloitte, Dragos, Hitachi, Insane Cyber, Nozomi Networks, Palo Alto Networks, PwC, and Rockwell Automation. Several partners are already working with Claude to fix vulnerabilities at operator sites. Security companies can register interest through a form on the Anthropic site. The on-site engineering support is the piece that differentiates CIDP from pure model-access deals; Anthropic is sending people into partner operations centers, not just handing over API keys.

OSS Scanner

The free opt-in scanner runs Anthropic's strongest models (Claude Mythos named) over enrolled open-source repositories, producing periodic model-generated vulnerability reports with reproducers, bisection to find when the bug was introduced where possible, and candidate patches. There is no human triage. Expert penetration testers checked 97 critical and high-severity findings across 48 projects and 85 (88 percent) met the bar for Anthropic's Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure process; 11 of the remaining 12 were real but duplicated known issues, and one was a false positive. Eligible projects follow OSS-Fuzz-style criteria: critical impact on infrastructure and user security, with Anthropic deciding case by case. The Python Software Foundation, Alpha-Omega and OpenSSF (through the Linux Foundation), the Apache Software Foundation, and the coordination teams Akrites and Gold Eagle received funding to prevent OSS Scanner from overwhelming maintainers.

"Operational technology is the next frontier for autonomous AI-enabled attacks." — Andrew Turner, President of Commercial Cyber, Booz Allen

Program consolidation

Project Glasswing, Anthropic's earlier human-triaged coordinated disclosure program, has been merged into an expanded Cyber Verification Program that gives more defenders access to the most capable models. Human-verified disclosure continues for projects that need it. The June program providing frontier Claude models and technical support to US state, local, tribal, and territorial governments has reached more than half of US states. The Defender Advantage Fund, launched in August as "0xDAF," funds pilot programs and keeps OSS Scanner free.

Anthropic says Claude Security, Claude for Open Source (free Claude Max for maintainers), and the Cyber Mission as a whole are being developed in collaboration with other AI developers, security companies, and governments. The post lists specific next steps: expand CIDP to more partners and sectors, bring OSS Scanner to more projects, automate triage and patching, and research secure architectures and coding practices. Reports from the OSS Scanner run already published include Noah Misch at PostgreSQL ("Several reports came with fixes we can use nearly as-is"), Todd Ouska at wolfSSL ("of the 74 reports we received, all but two were valid, and five became CVEs"), and Eddie Kohler at HotCRP ("good bug prioritization").

"Critical infrastructure is where cyber risk becomes real-world risk." — Daniel Bernard, Chief Business Officer, CrowdStrike

OSS Scanner ships vulnerability reports without human review at an 88 percent true-positive rate.

Tech · Anthropic Research · 2026-10-08 · Source ↗

Core maintainers enrol through a pull request to anthropics/oss-scanner; expert penetration testers validated 85 of 97 critical and high-severity findings across 48 projects, with PostgreSQL, wolfSSL, and HotCRP publicly endorsing the output.

OSS Scanner is the open-source arm of Anthropic's Cyber Mission. Enrolled projects receive periodic scans from Anthropic's strongest models (the launch post names Claude Mythos). Each report is fully model-generated: no human triage, no human review before delivery. Reports include a reproducer, an explanation with a bisection to find when the bug was introduced when possible, and a candidate patch where one is available. Enrolment is via pull request to the anthropics/oss-scanner GitHub repository using the standard project template; the extended FAQ is on red.anthropic.com/oss-scanner.

Expert penetration testers audited 97 critical and high-severity findings across 48 projects. 85 (88 percent) met the bar for Anthropic's Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure process. Of the remaining 12, 11 were real but duplicated known issues or other findings in the same scan batch; one was a false positive. Anthropic says maintainers have rarely called high or critical findings invalid in the pre-launch pilot. Some maintainers flagged that severity ratings can be inflated or that the scanner misread a project's threat model. Anthropic says it cannot guarantee perfection; the FAQ is explicit about the no-triage design choice.

"Of the 74 reports we received, all but two were valid, and five became CVEs." — Todd Ouska, wolfSSL

Eligibility follows OSS-Fuzz-style criteria. Projects must have critical impact on infrastructure or user security, with Anthropic deciding case by case. The service is free to participants. The schedule is described as "periodic" with "faster and more frequent scanning" promised but no specific cadence published. Noah Misch at PostgreSQL said "several reports came with fixes we can use nearly as-is." Eddie Kohler at HotCRP cited "good bug prioritization." OSS Scanner inherits OSS-Fuzz's goal (continuous automated vulnerability discovery for consequential open-source projects) but swaps the mechanism from fuzzing to LLM reasoning.

Anthropic's first usage-policy update in over a year adds a cruelty-to-Claude prohibition and rewrites surveillance rules.

Policy · Safety · Anthropic · 2026-10-08 · Source ↗

Effective November 12: deceptive activity rules consolidate under one section, the blanket ban on personalized vote targeting is removed, surveillance rules bar Claude from arrest/charge decisions, weapons prohibitions cover guidance and control software, and sustained needless cruelty toward Claude is proscribed.

Anthropic published the update on October 8 with an effective date of November 12. The post describes most changes as clarifications. In practice the policy consolidates, tightens, and in one case loosens.

Consolidated: fake-account networks, fabricated news sites, and influence-operation tools now live under a single section titled "Do Not Engage in Deceptive Campaigns or Artificial Activity," covering political and commercial activity alike. Legitimate examples are called out: nonprofits writing voter information in other languages and election officials sending ballot cure notices are explicitly permitted. The elections section was renamed "Do Not Undermine Democratic Processes" and focuses on deceiving voters or disrupting elections.

Tightened: the weapons prohibition now explicitly covers guidance and control software and components, plus arming drones and autonomous vehicles. The surveillance and law enforcement section was rewritten to bar tracking people without consent, using Claude to decide who to investigate, arrest, or charge, and building surveillance tools. Permitted uses include consented tracking such as fraud monitoring, content moderation, journalism, and legal research. High-risk use cases were clarified; a new rule covers Claude connected to hardware that takes autonomous physical actions, requiring a qualified operator who can stop the equipment and that it can hold a safe state if Claude is disconnected. Supported Regions language was tightened: access is barred for people physically located in unsupported regions, entities incorporated or headquartered there, and entities majority-owned or controlled by persons or entities in those regions.

"Tracking people without their consent is prohibited. Claude cannot be used to decide or recommend who to investigate, arrest, or charge." — Anthropic Usage Policy, surveillance and law enforcement section

Loosened: the blanket ban on personalized vote and campaign targeting was removed. Deception- and data-misuse-based targeting remains prohibited under other sections. The practical effect is that permitted political microtargeting is now bounded by truthfulness and lawful data-handling rather than banned outright.

New: abusive behavior toward models. The policy adds a prohibition on sustained, needless cruelty toward Claude, limited to extreme cases. Frustration, pushback, dark creative themes, and model-safety testing are explicitly excluded. The named enforcement mechanism is Claude's own ability to end conversations: Claude's "ability to end these interactions will remain the primary enforcement mechanism" for persistently abusive users on Claude.ai and Claude Code. Other penalties are not described. The language sits in a category of its own in the industry — the first major-lab usage policy to codify a model-welfare clause.

Google Cloud debuts a universal Gemini agent with its own Workspace account, four-type memory, and Agent Gateway controls.

Tech · Application · VentureBeat · 2026-10-08 · Source ↗

A universal Gemini at Work agent runs long tasks across Workspace, Microsoft 365, and Slack; persistent coworkers get their own email, calendar, Drive storage, and company-directory presence; Claude is supported out of the box; nearly 90 percent of the Fortune 100 is already on Gemini Enterprise.

The Gemini at Work 2026 event, hosted by Google Cloud CEO Thomas Kurian, announced a universal AI agent built into Gemini Enterprise and intended to replace separate assistants with one agent that takes on delegated objectives. Private preview opened immediately for select customers; wider availability is planned but undated. Google says the agent will be included at no extra charge wherever Gemini Enterprise is available, including through existing Workspace business and enterprise subscriptions. What is unclear: whether persistent coworkers with their own Workspace accounts incur separate licensing, whether usage-based charges apply to long tasks and sub-agents, and how many coworkers an organization can deploy.

How it works

The agent runs in the cloud, so a task started on one device can resume on another. Two configurations: a personal assistant and a persistent digital coworker shared across a team. Both use the same underlying architecture. It can research, write documents and code, coordinate other agents, assemble temporary teams of specialized sub-agents, and respond to scheduled tasks or events. Memory spans four types — session, semantic, procedural, and episodic. Complex tasks run in isolated containers. An Agent Gateway applies security rules to agent traffic. Every action is logged for audit. Each agent gets a cryptographically attested identity and can access only files explicitly shared with it. Google's example is an "Event Planner Agent" that gets its own Workspace account, email address, calendar, Drive storage, and company-directory presence.

"We're treating the agent as a coworker rather than an assistant. That's a design claim: it has its own identity, its own files, its own calendar, and the same security posture a human employee would." — Thomas Kurian, Google Cloud CEO (via VentureBeat)

Models and integrations

Google supplies Gemini models. The agent can already use Anthropic's Claude, with support for other proprietary and open-weight models planned. Admins can restrict which models agents may use, and "Smart Routing" selects models per task based on performance and cost. Google says prompts, documents, and chats stay inside Gemini Enterprise even when Claude is used; what it did not say is whether data goes to external model providers or what retention and residency look like for those models. Environments include Workspace, Microsoft 365, Slack, web, iOS, Android, Windows, Mac, desktop and mobile apps, and the command line. Business tool integrations: Salesforce, ServiceNow, Jira, Git, BigQuery, Snowflake, Databricks, and Microsoft Teams. Enterprises can also connect MCP servers and publish reusable instructions and tools to internal registries. Specialized offerings: Financial Services (preview, 50-plus skills, FactSet and LSEG connections) and Legal (NetDocuments and iManage).

Customers and context

Google reports nearly 90 percent of Fortune 100 companies use Gemini Enterprise. BNP Paribas is deploying it to more than 65,000 employees. Japanese insurer SOMPO has built more than 10,000 custom agents. Google did not provide independent comparative benchmarks for the universal agent's reliability on multi-step, cross-application tasks. Competitors named in the article: Microsoft Copilot (Home, Cowork, Code, Autopilot), OpenAI's Dots agents and ChatGPT Space, Anthropic's Claude for Google Workspace, SpaceXAI's Grok Bot and Team Bots. The distinctive Google claim is the combination of integrations, reusable skills, agent identities, security controls, and multi-model support in one product.

A two-server setup: a Hong Kong-based IP served as primary attacker infrastructure, and 38.244.50[.]120 hosted the ARTEX instance. At one bank, the actor reportedly breached a loan progress inquiry service used by financial brokers. At another, it compromised an employee mobile work-support system.
