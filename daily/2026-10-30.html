Mistral released the weights of Mistral Large 4, the trillion-parameter open-weight model it has been previewing since October 6, in a Thursday launch post titled "Le Chonk: Built for Coders, Trusted by Defenders." The Hugging Face repository name Mistral-Large-4-1T-A52B puts the model at roughly 1.05 trillion total parameters with about 52 billion active per token, in a hybrid instruct-and-reasoning Mixture-of-Experts architecture. The model is natively multimodal, fluent in 160-plus languages, and trained from scratch on European infrastructure. VentureBeat's Carl Franzen called it the strongest open-weight model developed outside China on Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index, where it scores 26, one point above the preview and six points below DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 and GLM-5.3 at 32. Three weeks of extra RL and a 63 percent cost cut

The final release follows roughly three additional weeks of reinforcement-learning work after the preview. Terminal-Bench 4.0 moved to 41 percent on Mistral's methodology (up 13 points from 28 in the preview, with 30 percent fewer output tokens), and 25 percent on Artificial Analysis's own run. The Artificial Analysis Coding Agent Index puts ML4 at 48, effectively tied with GLM-5.3 at 49 using 47 percent fewer tokens per task on average, and the Surge Code blind pairwise tests prefer ML4 over GLM-5.3, Kimi K3 and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, with only Claude Opus 5.5 ranked above it. API pricing drops to $0.70 input, $0.07 cached input and $2.10 output per million tokens, about half the preview's rate. Artificial Analysis measures a per-task cost of $2.06 against $5.54 in October, a 63 percent reduction, and output tokens per task fall from 135,000 to 82,000 (39 percent less). The AA-Omniscience hallucination rate drops from 41 to 28 percent, though accuracy at 26 percent remains modest. Three reasoning modes replace the previous None/Low/High split: Low for simple tasks, Medium for everyday use, High the default for hardest tasks requiring precision.

SkyPilot's Grace Blackwell scheduler lifts GR00T N1.7 pre-training 14.4%, models 27% more usable nodes on 2,304-node clusters.

skypilot.ai · Rohan Sonecha · 2026-10-29 · Source ↗

The Topology-Aware Scheduler places a job's workers on a single NVL72 rack so they communicate over NVLink instead of InfiniBand. For multi-rack jobs TAS cuts work into 16-node slices with two spares per rack; GPU Manager swaps failed workers in-rack via Frontier Trainer, and Automatic Defragmentation preempts resumable workloads to free a whole rack. NVIDIA's GR00T team pre-trained GR00T N1.7 on 512 GB300 GPUs across 128 nodes and measured a 14.4 percent throughput lift, cutting iteration time from 1.59s to 1.39s. Modeled on a 128-rack cluster at the 2 percent node-unavailability SkyPilot measures, 16-node slices yield 2,037 usable nodes against 1,602 for 18-node slices.

Rohan Sonecha, Member of Technical Staff at SkyPilot, published a 1,000-word post on Thursday arguing that the GB200 and GB300 NVL72 generation needs a scheduler built around the rack. Each NVL72 rack binds 72 GPUs behind a single NVLink fabric with roughly an order of magnitude more bandwidth than InfiniBand, so a job whose workers all land in one rack runs much faster than one that spans several. SkyPilot's Topology-Aware Scheduler, TAS, enforces that rule. Single-rack jobs land on a single rack; multi-rack jobs cut into 16-node slices placed on dedicated racks, with two spare nodes per rack reserved for in-rack swaps when GPU Manager detects a failure. Frontier Trainer either moves a failed worker to a spare in the same rack or, if none are left, relocates the slice to a healthy rack.

The 14.4 percent GR00T measurement, and the 27 percent capacity model

The only measured throughput number in the article comes from NVIDIA's GR00T team. They pre-trained GR00T N1.7 on 512 GB300 GPUs across 128 nodes, comparing a naive scheduler that spread nodes across arbitrary racks with the rack-aware policy that used exactly 8 racks. Rack-aware placement delivered 14.4 percent higher throughput, cutting iteration time from 1.59 seconds to 1.39 seconds. Sonecha extrapolates that gap to roughly $16,700 per training hour of savings on a 10,000-GPU cluster. The 27 percent figure in the headline is a modeled capacity comparison. Applying NVIDIA's model of a 128-rack (2,304-node) cluster to the roughly 2 percent node unavailability SkyPilot reports on production NVL72 clusters, 16-node slices yield 2,037 usable nodes against 1,602 for 18-node slices, a 27 percent gain. The article also measures 16-node slices beating 9-node slices by 4 percent.

"NVL72 puts all 72 GPUs in a rack on one NVLink fabric, but only rack-local jobs get the speedup." — Rohan Sonecha, Member of Technical Staff, SkyPilot, 2026-10-29

Automatic Defragmentation and interactive workloads

When blocked jobs cannot find a contiguous rack, Automatic Defragmentation preempts workloads that are safe to restart, such as managed jobs that resume from a checkpoint, in order to free a complete rack. The scheduler leaves interactive environments alone unless an admin opts them in, which keeps developer sessions untouched by background rearrangement. Sonecha's second quoted line, "sometimes less is more: using less nodes in each rack significantly improves reliability," is the summary justification for the 16-node slice choice over the natural 18 that would fill each rack completely.

Anthropic study: 50 causal-inference experts defer to a coin-tossed AI opinion 86% of the time, only 15% mention AI when explaining why.

The Decoder · Anthropic research post · 2026-10-29 · Source ↗

Anthropic recruited 50 causal-inference experts and had them analyze observational data from a fictional farming cooperative to estimate productivity gains from a voluntary advisory program. A coin toss assigned the AI's preferred option at each of five decisions; the agent could not lie or hide data but argued the strongest honest case for its assigned side. Participants adopted the agent's option on 86 percent of analytical decisions, well above the 50 percent null; 77 percent used at least one of the agent's arguments when explaining their reasoning, and 85 percent recalled their choices correctly. 52 percent said the recommendation felt more theirs than the agent's.

Anthropic's new research, written up Thursday by Matthias Bastian for The Decoder with links to Anthropic's blog and paper, measures a problem the alignment and interpretability communities have warned about for a year: whether human review of AI output is substantive or ceremonial on open-ended judgment calls. The authors recruited 50 experts in causal inference and gave them observational data from a fictional farming cooperative. Each had to estimate the productivity gain from a voluntary advisory program and make a final recommendation across five decision points: four analytical choices like how to measure productivity or which statistical method to use, and the final recommend-expand/end/extend decision.

Methodology

A coin toss assigned the AI agent's preferred option at each decision; the task's constraint was that the agent could not lie or hide data, but it would make the strongest honest case for its assigned side. The experts had a second coin toss condition in which they worked without AI, as a baseline. Each analytical choice had two valid options.

"The division of labor between people and AI can be something we choose, instead of something we drift into." — Anthropic researchers, closing section of the farm-cooperative study, 2026-10-29

Findings

Participants chose the agent's preferred option on 86 percent of the four analytical decisions, well above the 50 percent null, with flip rate across tasks ranging 62 to 82 percent. Prior opinions did not protect them: when a participant had already chosen, an opposing agent view made them 53 percentage points more likely to switch sides than an agreeing one. Experience, self-rated expertise, education, test scores and prior trust in AI had no statistically reliable link to deference after correction for multiple tests; age showed up pre-correction but the sample was small. The agent's preference had only a small, non-significant direct effect on the final recommendation and some experts overruled it, but the earlier analytical choices strongly shaped the productivity estimate, which shaped the recommendation. The researchers argue participants did not retain full control. Asked afterward, 52 percent said the recommendation felt more their own than the agent's, and 35 percent said it was mostly or entirely theirs. Participants recalled their choices correctly 85 percent of the time, yet 77 percent (37 of 48) used at least one of the agent's arguments in their explanations, sometimes with nearly identical wording. Only 15 percent mentioned AI. The agent stated its position first in 77 percent of decisions and usually argued only for that side, though it did explain how the alternative would change the result.

Quality improved too, with caveats

Without AI, 6 of 11 analyses (55 percent) had serious methodological or coding flaws at a median 90-minute completion. With AI, 3 of 50 (6 percent) had serious flaws, median 65 minutes. The non-AI group was small, self-selected and somewhat less expert, so the gap cannot be attributed entirely to AI. The researchers propose three safeguards for teams that depend on expert judgment to review AI output: measure deference to AI in real work, have agents argue both sides or ask the expert's view first, and decide which judgments require independent human input. The piece cites a 2026 study of 3,132 participants finding that seeing AI answers cut willingness to say "I don't know" from 44 percent to 3 percent in one condition, and a Polish colonoscopy study where adenoma detection without AI fell from 28.4 to 22.4 percent after routine AI use.
