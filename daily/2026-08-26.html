OpenAI published Jalapeño benchmarks 24 hours before Nvidia's earnings, claiming up to 4.1x lower inference latency than Blackwell

Head of Hardware Richard Ho posted the first SemiAnalysis InferenceX results for OpenAI's Broadcom-designed inference ASIC on Tuesday morning, one day before Nvidia's Q2 FY27 print after the close on Wednesday. Each Jalapeño chip runs at 13.4 petaFLOPS in MXFP4, packs 216 GB of HBM4 with 15.4 TB/s of bandwidth, and pulls roughly 700 W; a rack aggregates 128 accelerators to 1.7 exaFLOPS of 4-bit compute, 27.5 TB of HBM4 and just under 2 PB/s of memory bandwidth. Against a comparable Blackwell rack, OpenAI reports 1.5x to 1.9x more AI work per watt at peak throughput, 1.7x to 3.6x lower end-to-end latency, and 2.1x to 4.1x higher performance on ultra-low-latency interactive workloads. Ho conceded the Blackwell and MI400 racks still deliver 1.46x to 2x more raw compute per rack and up to 12 percent more memory, and said Jalapeño ships in "very small volumes" at the end of 2026 with meaningful volume in 2027.

TechCrunch · Devin Coldewey · 2026-08-25 · Source ↗

OpenAI Head of Hardware Richard Ho posted the first public benchmark numbers for Jalapeño, the company's Broadcom-designed inference ASIC, on Tuesday morning. The publication timing is not accidental: Nvidia reports Q2 FY27 earnings after the close on Wednesday, August 26, against a $92 billion consensus and a set of investor questions that have shifted this quarter from "can Nvidia keep pace with demand" to "who else can serve inference on a comparable performance envelope."

"The bottom line is that the results show a very, very significant performance advance over state of the art." — Richard Ho, Head of Hardware, OpenAI

Per-chip and per-rack numbers

Each Jalapeño accelerator runs at 13.4 petaFLOPS in MXFP4, carries 216 GB of HBM4 with 15.4 TB/s of memory bandwidth, and dissipates roughly 700 watts. A single Jalapeño rack fits 128 accelerators, aggregating 1.7 exaFLOPS of 4-bit compute, 27.5 TB of HBM4 and just under 2 PB/s of memory bandwidth. Ho was frank that Nvidia's Blackwell rack and AMD's MI400 rack still deliver 1.46x to 2x more raw compute per rack and up to 12 percent more memory. His argument is that raw compute is not the binding constraint on reasoning-model decode: memory bandwidth is. Jalapeño's rack provides 85 percent higher memory bandwidth than the Blackwell rack it compares against, and that bandwidth advantage is what carries the latency deltas Ho published.

What the SemiAnalysis InferenceX runs showed

On the SemiAnalysis InferenceX benchmark, Ho reported Jalapeño delivering 1.5x to 1.9x more inference work per watt at peak throughput than the current state of the art, 1.7x to 3.6x lower end-to-end latency, and 2.1x to 4.1x higher performance on ultra-low-latency interactive workloads. The interactive-latency delta is the one OpenAI cares most about. Voice, coding-agent inner loops, and any workload that pipes a user through more than a few thousand serial tokens per turn is bounded by tail latency on decode, and the Jalapeño design collapses prefill and decode onto the same die so KV-cache state stays local rather than shuttling across an interconnect.

"Jalapeño can serve more AI work per unit of power, while also returning responses more quickly." — Richard Ho, Head of Hardware, OpenAI

Nine months from design to silicon

The design-cycle disclosure is the second headline of the post. Ho said the team went from initial design to silicon in nine months, roughly half the historic mainstream ASIC cadence, and that AI-generated RTL beat human-written implementations by 1.5x to 1.8x on selected blocks. The credit lands on OpenAI's own frontier models. Jalapeño is captive silicon: OpenAI will not sell it. Deployment begins inside OpenAI's own fleet in "very small volumes" at the end of 2026, with meaningful volume slipping to 2027, and Broadcom is already engaged on Gen 2 and Gen 3.

The Nvidia framing

Nvidia's Wednesday print is expected to nearly double year-over-year revenue to $92.16 billion from $46.7 billion, and options markets are pricing a 5.4 percent post-print move in either direction, a $280 billion market-cap swing. That number is below the 6.5 percent implied ahead of the May report and Nvidia's 12-quarter historical average of 7.4 percent, which the options desks read as complacency. The Jalapeño post is a direct attempt to reset the terms of the Nvidia guidance debate. If OpenAI's largest customer is publishing benchmark decks 24 hours before the print showing 4.1x latency wins, the guidance question shifts from "how fast can Nvidia grow" to "how much of the inference stack is Nvidia going to keep."

"We designed Jalapeño to minimize data movement and communication delays. Model state, including the KV cache used while generating a response, can be explicitly placed and kept local while the system activates the right combination of compute, memory, and networking for each inference phase." — Richard Ho, Head of Hardware, OpenAI

Caveats on the benchmark itself

The SemiAnalysis InferenceX numbers are OpenAI-run and OpenAI-reported. Nvidia has not independently reproduced them, and the "current state of the art" baseline is not specified beyond "comparable Blackwell." AMD's MI400 pods, which ship in similar Ethernet-scale-up configurations, are outside the comparison entirely. Deployment volume is small enough at end-2026 that Jalapeño will not materially move OpenAI's Nvidia GPU procurement inside the year. The Cerebras Nexus CS-4 launch earlier in August posted comparable claims against Nvidia stacks on Humanity's Last Exam. The pattern is now firmly established: every frontier inference-compute vendor is going to publish OpenAI-scale benchmark packages before Nvidia's earnings prints, and OpenAI is the first Nvidia customer to do it with silicon it designed itself.

The first-person / third-person split

"Maintaining synchronized environmental sound and speech over long-horizon generation." — EchoWM paper, benchmarks section

AgentWeave paper cuts function-calling tokens 61 percent with deterministic pre-inference routing

arXiv · 2026-08-25 · Source ↗

Saurav Singla and three co-authors published AgentWeave on arXiv, arguing pre-inference routing beats fine-tuned self-routing. The system computes a bounded action space per query and reports 70.18 percent fewer tools presented, 61.70 percent fewer input tokens, 50.95 percent lower mean latency, and 6-of-48 successes against 0-of-48 for the baseline on BFCL V4.

The AgentWeave argument is that the tool-rich function-calling problem has been miscast. Rather than fine-tune models to route their own tool calls, put a deterministic pre-inference router in front of the model that narrows the visible action space before inference begins. The system builds four filter signals — eligibility, requirements, capability, and routing — and uses them to compute the bounded model-visible action space for each incoming request.

The BFCL V4 numbers

Evaluation runs against MadeAgents/Hammer2.1-1.5b on BFCL V4 multiple-function tasks (48 instances). Success rises to 6 of 48 problems from 0 of 48 for the baseline (McNemar p=0.03125). The efficiency deltas are the story: 70.18 percent fewer tools presented per query, 61.70 percent fewer input tokens consumed and 50.95 percent lower mean inference latency. The authors deliberately position the numbers as small-N and not a leaderboard submission, so the artifact is a proof of mechanism rather than a state-of-the-art claim.
