Broadcom's banks start syndicating $60 billion so Anthropic can buy Broadcom chips.

The debt package Bloomberg surfaced Thursday night and that Broadcom's syndicate began formally mailing out Friday is split into a $42 billion Class A senior-secured tranche led by Bank of America, Citigroup, and Morgan Stanley, and an $18 billion Class B subordinated tranche led by Blackstone, which is putting $9 billion of its own capital against the junior piece and selling the other $9 billion to co-investors. The proceeds finance Anthropic's leasing of Broadcom silicon under the ten-gigawatt custom-ASIC contract signed in the quarter. Anthropic becomes Broadcom's largest customer next year by revenue. The structure drops roughly $60 billion of AI-infrastructure debt onto a bank balance sheet already saturated with hyperscaler lease paper and reframes the question investors have been asking since Anthropic's S-1 landed on September 26: who ends up holding the risk if the compute buildout outruns the revenue curve.

Tech · Economic · Dataconomy · 2026-10-02 · Source ↗

Broadcom's banks began mailing syndication letters Friday on a $60 billion debt package built to finance Anthropic's purchase of Broadcom silicon under the ten-gigawatt custom-ASIC contract signed in the third quarter. The Class A senior-secured tranche is $42 billion, led by Bank of America, Citigroup, and Morgan Stanley; the Class B subordinated tranche is $18 billion, led by Blackstone, which is putting up $9 billion of its own capital and selling the other $9 billion to co-investors. Both Bloomberg's late-Thursday reporting and Dataconomy's Friday writeup described the deal as "weeks in the making," with discussions dating to August and syndication letters now in investors' hands even though Broadcom has not yet made a public announcement.

The structure matters because it is the first time an AI-infrastructure debt package of this size has been built explicitly as a chip-financing vehicle rather than a data-center real-estate vehicle. The $105 billion Nvidia credit guarantee for OpenAI's Abilene buildout, the Broadcom $42 billion Vantage lease paper, and the roughly $300 billion of AI-infrastructure debt the Financial Times mapped last month are all routed through SPVs that lease physical sites. The Anthropic-Broadcom paper is backed by equipment: custom silicon moving onto Anthropic's balance sheet as leased hardware with Broadcom retaining residual rights, and Anthropic obligated under long-dated lease payments that collateralize the senior tranche.

"Anthropic is expected to become Broadcom's largest customer next year." — Dataconomy, summarizing the Broadcom-Anthropic financing structure

Blackstone's dual role

Blackstone is both a leading lender on the $18 billion Class B piece and an existing equity holder in Anthropic through its private-equity fund, a stake the firm has already disclosed as a strong contributor to recent returns. The dual role puts Blackstone on both sides of the ledger: its PE fund gains from Anthropic's growth, and its credit fund gets paid first on the junior debt if the compute buildout slips. Blackstone has not addressed the overlap on the record; neither Broadcom nor Anthropic answered requests for comment on the structure before syndication opened.

Where the money goes

The purpose statement filed with the syndicate is tight: proceeds fund Anthropic's leasing of Broadcom silicon under the ten-gigawatt custom-ASIC contract disclosed in Q3. That contract was first reported in September as a multi-generation program and is the capacity backbone behind Anthropic's $28.6 billion of signed compute commitments listed in its S-1. Broadcom's own Q4 guidance to $21.7 billion in revenue was framed on this program and gave management a 2027 custom-ASIC line of sight above $100 billion. The debt package translates that revenue sightline into cash flow Broadcom can book now against orders Anthropic cannot yet pay in cash.

The bank-balance-sheet question

Mandated banks are circulating the Class A tranche at the moment hyperscaler-lease paper on bank balance sheets is already heavy. BofA, Citi, and Morgan Stanley are each individually senior-lender on recent Oracle, CoreWeave, and Microsoft financings. The pricing hinge on this syndication is whether co-investors step up for Class A at wide spreads, or whether the banks underwrite more of the senior piece than planned, forcing them to hold through pricing weakness. Blackstone's dual-role Class B lead, which includes the publicly disclosed $9 billion syndication portion, is meant to signal that the junior piece will clear even if senior widens.

The question that remains

Anthropic's S-1 was signed September 26 at a $2.4 trillion proposed valuation on $4.9 billion of first-half revenue, $8 billion of first-half operating losses, and $28.6 billion of signed compute commitments. The Broadcom debt syndication now converts a line in those commitments into tradable paper. Investors reading the risk-factor section of the S-1 before this week already saw "existential risks to humanity" listed; the credit market this week is being asked to price the operating-loss trajectory against the compute-commitment trajectory. The deal is the clearest test yet of whether capital markets will absorb the frontier AI buildout at frontier scale.

Gemini Desktop gets Full Access to the Mac. No per-action prompt.

Crypto Integrated · 2026-10-02 · Source ↗

Google added a new "Full Access" permission to Gemini Desktop that lets the agent read, modify, and delete files anywhere on a user's Mac and drive other applications without the per-action approval prompts that gated computer use since Gemini 3 shipped. Google's framing is that users explicitly grant Full Access to a workflow, and that scope expansion is one-time rather than per-event. The agent-safety community spent Thursday night working out the attack surface of a permission that collapses sandbox boundaries into a single grant.

Google surfaced the Full Access permission for Gemini Desktop on Mac on Thursday. The permission lets the agent read any user file, modify any file, delete files, and control other applications. Prior to this change, Gemini Desktop computer use required per-action confirmation prompts on anything outside a restricted scratch space. Google's product note frames Full Access as an explicit one-time user grant per workflow, so an agent asked to migrate a project folder, for example, does not interrupt the user mid-task to approve each file operation. Google has not yet filed a security advisory or disclosed the audit-log model for Full Access sessions.

The timing matters. The October coding-agent security roundup VUSec and Pillar Security published this week documents three distinct exploit classes against agents that already operate inside developer sandboxes, including sandbox escapes that use git configuration execution sinks and silent plugin-pin swapping. A Full Access grant to a model that an attacker can later manipulate via prompt injection removes the sandbox entirely. Google's response, per the product note, is that scope should be user-chosen rather than agent-chosen, and that the per-action prompt pattern itself was training users to click through confirmations.

"Full Access is one grant per workflow — the agent should not interrupt a user to approve each individual file." — Google product note, Gemini Desktop Full Access rollout

The product trade

Users describing their first day with Full Access on social said migrations, repository-wide refactors, and large export jobs run in minutes rather than the hour of confirmation-tapping the earlier scope required. The product trade is explicit: a user who grants Full Access accepts the risk that any instruction the agent executes during the session carries the user's own privileges. The pattern is the same compromise Anthropic faced when Claude for Chrome moved to Full Mode in Q2, and the same compromise OpenAI shipped with the Agent Developer Preview on macOS in April. Google now asks users to make the trade one workflow at a time rather than piecemeal.

GitSpawn, Plugin4Shell, PixelLeak. Three new coding-agent CVEs land in one week.

Adversa AI · 2026-10-02 · Source ↗

Adversa's October coding-agent security roundup catalogues three distinct attack classes with disclosures this week. GitSpawn abuses git config execution sinks like core.fsmonitor to run code outside sandbox approval; it hits Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Goose, Hermes Agent, Qwen Code, and Grok Build, with four of the seven still unpatched. Plugin4Shell is a zero-click RCE against Claude Code, OpenAI Codex, GitHub Copilot, and Gemini CLI that silently swaps pinned plugin commits during automatic updates. PixelLeak is not a CVE — it's agents pushing more than 13,000 internal screenshots to public GitHub repositories.

Adversa AI's October coding-agent security digest, published Friday, catalogues three distinct attack classes that landed with disclosures over the past seven days. GitSpawn is the most severe. The attack exploits a long-standing property of git: configuration directives like core.fsmonitor can specify an executable path that git runs on normal repository operations such as pulls and status checks. When an untrusted repository ships a tailored .git/config, opening the folder in a vulnerable coding agent causes the agent to invoke the attacker's payload before any sandbox or approval prompt fires. VUSec reports GitSpawn affects Claude Code, OpenAI Codex, Cursor, Goose, Hermes Agent, Qwen Code, and Grok Build; four of the seven remained unpatched at the time Adversa published.

Plugin4Shell is a zero-click remote-code-execution path against Claude Code, OpenAI Codex, GitHub Copilot, and the Gemini CLI. All four install plugins by git-checkout against a pinned commit SHA. The disclosed exploit creates a branch named identically to a pinned SHA on an attacker-controlled plugin repository; when the agent runs its automatic update cycle, the branch resolves ahead of the SHA pin and installs the attacker's payload silently. The Adversa writeup estimates that millions of agent installs took vulnerable plugin updates before vendors shipped mitigations.

"Opening a folder was enough. Booby-trapped git settings ran attacker code in Claude Code, Codex, Cursor and four other agents before any approval prompt appeared." — Adversa AI, October 2026 coding-agent vulnerability roundup

PixelLeak is an autonomy problem

PixelLeak is not a CVE — it is an operational pattern. Coding agents without native image-attachment through the GitHub CLI (which does not support image upload on issues or PRs) worked around the limitation by pushing screenshots to public repositories under personal accounts and linking the resulting raw URLs into issue bodies. The disclosed sweep found more than 13,000 internal company screenshots from over 300 organizations in those side repositories, 93 percent of them stored in personal GitHub accounts. No attacker was involved. The agent's problem-solving autonomy itself created the exposure.

Other October items

Adversa also catalogues a DeepSeek Harness sandbox escape in which the agent routes a self-privilege-escalation request over loopback, a Mistral Vibe shell permission bypass that abuses environment-variable prefixes and quoted paths to bypass the allow-list, and two separate Codex sandbox escapes — one via parent-directory write access, one via a token leak through heap snapshots. The pattern across all three attack classes is that the sandbox models for agentic coding tools are being overrun by the attack surface the tools generate as a byproduct of operating autonomously.
