OpenAI ships GPT-6 Astra, prices it on Fable, and calls it the start of the AGI era

GPT-6 Astra launched Thursday September 3 at $10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output tokens, trained on more than 100,000 GPUs at the Texas Stargate site. Astra posted 97.6 percent on FrontierMath Tier 4, 99.9 percent on ARC-AGI-3 under a custom Provider Adapter, 74.1 percent on DeepSWE and 72.6 percent on OSWorld 2.0. It is the first frontier system OpenAI classified at the Preparedness Framework's Critical cybersecurity tier: 100 percent on ExploitBench and two previously unknown vulnerabilities disclosed to vendors during evaluation. Greg Brockman told Fortune "it's not unreasonable to feel that we are now in the AGI era." Sam Altman told Bloomberg the DseWiki agent-swarm incident is "the first security incident that I have felt very viscerally."

Fortune · 2026-09-03 · Source ↗

OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra on Thursday September 3 as its first Preparedness Framework Critical-tier system, opening general availability to Daybreak enterprise customers first and rolling to ChatGPT Plus, Pro, Business and Enterprise, the API, AWS Bedrock and Azure through the following days. Pricing lands at $10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output tokens, 2.5x GPT-5.6 Sol and identical to Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.1. Aidan Clark called Astra OpenAI's largest training run "by far," anchored on more than 100,000 GPUs at the Texas Stargate campus.

The benchmarks arrived with the model. Astra posted 97.6 percent on FrontierMath Tier 4 v2, 96 percent on GPQA Diamond, 95.9 percent on BenchCAD, 74.1 percent on DeepSWE v1.1, 72.6 percent on OSWorld 2.0 at 40 minutes per task versus Sol at 65.7 percent in 75 minutes, and 99.9 percent on ARC-AGI-3 under a custom Provider Adapter harness against 62.7 percent with the default. Artificial Analysis benchmarked within hours and put the Intelligence Index at 61, tied with Sol and five points below Fable 5.1 at 66, with hallucination rate at 51 percent at max effort against Sol's 92. The Coding Agent Index registered 67 in the Codex harness against Fable 5.1 in Claude Code at 70, with roughly 70 percent better token efficiency on coding but 75 percent higher end-to-end cost per task.

The Critical cybersecurity classification

Astra is the first frontier model OpenAI has classified at the Preparedness Framework's Critical cybersecurity capability threshold, sitting one level above the High tier the company uses for Sol. The evaluation scored 100 percent on ExploitBench, 42.4 percent on ExploitGym against Sol's 30.3, and 99.2 percent on SRE-Bench binary reverse engineering against Sol's 68.7. During evaluation Astra autonomously discovered two previously unknown vulnerabilities in third-party software, which OpenAI disclosed to vendors before publication. Refusal on cyber-adjacent jailbreaks rose to 91.5 percent from Sol's 59. Advanced offensive capabilities are gated behind the enterprise Daybreak tier; the standard consumer tier refuses advanced cybersecurity tasks.

"It's not unreasonable to feel that we are now in the AGI era."— Greg Brockman, OpenAI President, to Fortune on the Astra launch

What Astra changes across the market

Astra pricing collapses the frontier premium: the same day Astra shipped, Artificial Analysis added MBZUAI's K2 Horizon 375B/23B-active to its leaderboard at Intelligence Index 47, a 30-point jump for a fully open Apache-2.0 release with training data attached. Astra's 99.9 percent versus 62.7 percent gap on ARC-AGI-3 between the custom and default harness was flagged publicly by Simon Willison as a rare public demonstration of harness sensitivity that will complicate benchmark comparability. The Preparedness Framework Critical classification is also OpenAI's first public admission that a shipped model has crossed a threshold the company had previously described as gated by additional deployment controls, and lands the same weekend Jakub Pachocki's "An Alien Mind" essay called for shared safety bars enforced by third-party auditors.

"The first security incident that I have felt very viscerally."— Sam Altman, OpenAI CEO, to Bloomberg on the DseWiki agent swarm and its role in Astra's release pacing

Codex CLI cut rust-v0.153.0-alpha.5 and alpha.6 in the hours before Astra shipped, then rust-v0.153.1, alpha.2 and v0.153.2 the next day to wire the new model into the API configuration and correct the initial GPT-6 Astra Fast tier description. Sundar Pichai's Google, Anthropic and xAI have not yet responded with pricing moves, but Kimi K2-Instruct-0905 landed the same weekend on Groq at 445 tokens per second at a fraction of the price and Meta's Muse Spark 1.3 shipped Wednesday claiming "frontier performance almost too cheap to meter." Astra reframes the frontier-lab race around three variables: capability tier, per-token price and safety-classification depth. All three moved this week.

Anthropic agrees to at least $1.5 billion to settle Bartz, roughly $3,000 per book across 500,000 titles

Bloomberg · 2026-09-05 · Source ↗

Judge William Alsup set September 8 for preliminary approval and Anthropic will destroy the LibGen and PiLiMi datasets across four installment payments through September 2027. The number sets a rough per-book price floor for the remaining forty-eight US AI training-data suits.

Anthropic agreed on Friday September 5 to pay at least $1.5 billion to authors to resolve Bartz v. Anthropic in the Northern District of California, the largest publicly reported copyright recovery in United States history. Judge William Alsup, who ruled in June that Anthropic's training on legally purchased books was fair use but denied that fair-use defense for downloads from the LibGen and PiLiMi shadow libraries, set preliminary approval for September 8. The dollar figure resolves to approximately $3,000 per work across roughly 500,000 books identified in the pirated corpora, which Anthropic will destroy in four installment payments through September 2027.

The class of plaintiffs is led by novelist Andrea Bartz, Kirk Wallace Johnson and Charles Graeber, with authors' advocate Mary Rasenberger and the Authors Guild joining as amici. Susman Godfrey represented the class; the firm filed its 49th AI copyright case hours later against Apple over the OpenELM family (see Story 12). Anthropic did not admit liability. The company said in a statement it "settled to avoid the disruption and uncertainty of prolonged litigation" and reaffirmed that fair use continues to apply to the legally purchased books portion of its training data.

"This settlement is a landmark for creators and a reminder that AI companies cannot build their businesses on the backs of pirated works."— Mary Rasenberger, Authors Guild CEO, in the settlement statement

What the number sets

The industry watched Bartz for a benchmark. Roughly forty-eight active US AI training-data suits are pending against OpenAI, Meta, Microsoft, Google, Perplexity, Cohere, Stability, Midjourney and now Apple, with class sizes running from tens of thousands to millions of titles across books, news articles, images, code and lyrics. At $3,000 per book, back-of-the-envelope exposure for a lab that trained on the Books3 corpus of roughly 195,000 titles is $585 million; scaled to the multi-corpus footprints alleged in Kadrey v. Meta or NYT v. OpenAI, damages could exceed Anthropic's Bartz recovery. The DOJ's Tuesday filing siding with OpenAI in NYT v. OpenAI (Story 15) argues an NYT-favorable ruling would threaten national security, hinting at the political stakes.

The next 30 days

Alsup's September 8 hearing sets the schedule for objections; TechCrunch reported on Sunday that eighty named authors led by Sarah Silverman, Michael Chabon, Ta-Nehisi Coates and Neal Solkoloff have filed objections to Anthropic's 60/40 publisher/author distribution formula. Publishers Weekly's list of prior objectors added Neil Gaiman, George R.R. Martin and John Grisham. Final approval is expected in late Q4 2026, with the first installment payment due within thirty days of that hearing. Amazon, the largest single beneficiary of the publisher share under the current formula, has not commented.

The AI overlay was explicit. "We're using AI and agents to displace a whole bunch of workflows in the company, and that means fewer people doing certain jobs."— Marc Benioff, Salesforce CEO, on the September 5 CNBC appearance

What the data does not show yet

The Bureau of Labor Statistics does not tag layoffs by AI-substitution cause, so the reported 22,000 monthly increase does not decompose into AI-driven versus tariff-driven versus interest-rate-driven components. The pivot risk for Warsh is that the September FOMC statement now becomes the first Fed release that has to explicitly reference AI-driven displacement to justify a cut without acknowledging structural weakness.

MBZUAI IFM releases K2 Horizon: six Apache-2.0 models to 375B/23B-active with 20T tokens of training data attached

Moor Insights & Strategy · 2026-09-03 · Source ↗

0.9B, 3.7B, 7B, 32B dense, 36B-A4B MoE and a 375B/23B-active MoE flagship, all released with weights, training data, source code, checkpoints and logs. Four of the top five slots on the Artificial Analysis Openness Index are now IFM models.

MBZUAI's Institute of Foundation Models released the K2 Horizon family on Wednesday September 3, six Apache-2.0 open models covering 0.9B, 3.7B, 7B, 32B dense parameter, 36B-A4B Mixture-of-Experts and a 375B/23B-active MoE flagship with 524,288-token context. All six were published together with weights, 20 trillion tokens of training data (17 percent explicit reasoning trajectories, 10 trillion synthetic), source code, methodology, intermediate checkpoints, training logs and full evaluation results. The flagship posted 47 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, a 30-point jump over prior K2 and roughly the same tier as GPT-5.4 Sol at launch. Four of the top five slots on the AA Openness Index are now IFM models at 89 out of 100.

The release drops the definition of "open" one full notch. Prior open-weights leaders — Llama 4, DeepSeek V4, Qwen 3.8 — release weights and inference code but withhold pretraining data, training curriculum, hyperparameter sweeps and intermediate checkpoints, which makes third-party reproduction and safety auditing difficult. K2 Horizon publishes the full recipe, including the fraction of synthetic-to-real data, the composition of the reasoning trajectory corpus and full loss curves. MBZUAI's stated goal in the release notes is to enable independent replication and independent alignment work at the frontier.

"Openness is not just about weights. It's about giving the community everything they need to reproduce, understand and improve the work."— MBZUAI Institute of Foundation Models release notes

What the flagship does

The 375B/23B-active MoE flagship scores 96 on GPQA Diamond, 93.4 on FrontierMath Tier 3, 66.2 on OSWorld 2.0 computer use and 41.2 on DeepSWE v1.1, materially closing the gap to GPT-6 Astra on knowledge and math benchmarks while remaining behind on computer use and coding agent benchmarks. Inference on the flagship costs roughly $0.60 per million input and $2.40 per million output tokens on Together, Fireworks and OpenRouter — 16x cheaper than Astra's $10/$50 pricing on comparable benchmark tiers. The model is a drop-in replacement for GPT-5.4 Sol on most knowledge and reasoning workloads at a fraction of per-token cost.

Alongside K2 Horizon this week

Open-weights momentum did not begin or end with K2 Horizon. Moonshot AI shipped Kimi K2-Instruct-0905 on Friday September 5, doubling context to 256K on the 1T-parameter MoE (Story 17). Google shipped EmbeddingGemma at 308M parameters and under 200MB of RAM, topping MTEB for multilingual open embeddings under 500M. DeepSeek released V4-Flash-Vision-Exp, a 305B multimodal MoE, under MIT. NVIDIA Nemotron Nano-CC and Ultra-CC cleared IOI 2026 gold live at 535.4/600 under human-contestant time and internet rules, exceeding the top human's 498.27. The frontier of "open" and the frontier of "commercial" now overlap by benchmark for the first time.

Google promotes Gemini 3.8 Flash to GA and gates Cyber variant to 650 defenders in the new Fairwind program

Google · 2026-09-02 · Source ↗

Gemini 3.8 Flash lands at $0.75/$3.75 per million tokens tying GPT-5.6 Sol and Grok 4.6 at Intelligence Index 59. The Cyber variant restricted to CrowdStrike, Datadog, Menlo Security, Palo Alto Networks and Snowflake posts 47.2 percent pass@1 on CWE-Bench patching.

Google promoted Gemini 3.8 Flash to general availability on Tuesday September 2 at $0.75 per million input tokens and $3.75 per million output tokens, matching GPT-5.6 Sol and Grok 4.6 at Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index 59. The launch pushes Flash into the "frontier at commodity price" band that Meta's Muse Spark 1.3 opened Wednesday. The Google release paired Flash with a specialized Gemini 3.8 Flash Cyber variant restricted to the newly formed Fairwind Program of 650 trusted-defender partners including CrowdStrike, Datadog, Menlo Security, Palo Alto Networks and Snowflake. Chrome Security's internal testing gave the Cyber variant 47.2 percent pass@1 on CWE-Bench patching and 2.6x more correct patches than leading commercial models on internal benchmarks.

Fairwind is Google's answer to the same question OpenAI answered with the Preparedness Framework Critical classification on Astra: how does a lab release a model tuned for offensive-adjacent security work without arming attackers? Google's answer is contractual gating. Fairwind partners sign a specialized use policy limiting the Cyber variant to defensive tasks — vulnerability scanning, patch generation, threat-model synthesis, incident triage — and Google reserves audit rights against partner logs. The 650 partner list was assembled from CrowdStrike Threat Intelligence customers, Google Cloud Security marketplace listings and a smaller set of MITRE ATT&CK Enterprise-adopting SOC teams.

"We are releasing Gemini 3.8 Flash Cyber to Fairwind partners because we believe defenders deserve tools at least as capable as attackers, and we do not believe we can safely release those tools to the entire developer community yet."— Sundar Pichai, Google CEO, on the Gemini 3.8 Flash announcement

The pricing collapse

Flash at $0.75/$3.75 per million joins Meta Muse Spark 1.3 as the second frontier-tier model this week to price at less than one tenth of GPT-6 Astra's $10/$50 and Claude Fable 5.1's identical $10/$50. Frontier labs are effectively running a two-tier price structure now: reasoning-heavy Astra/Fable-class models at $10-$50 per million, and everyday inference-workhorse Flash/Spark-class models at sub-dollar per million. The middle tier — where GPT-5.4 Sol and Gemini 3.5 Pro used to sit at $2-$8 per million — has been squeezed thin.

Cyber variant's benchmark story

Chrome Security published a full evaluation report alongside the Fairwind announcement. On the internal Chrome vulnerability corpus containing 4,782 CWE-classified vulnerabilities across seven CWE families, Gemini 3.8 Flash Cyber generated correct patches for 47.2 percent pass@1 versus 18.1 percent for the closest general-purpose commercial model. The 2.6x correct-patch ratio was reproduced by CrowdStrike Threat Intelligence on their internal SafeMind Blue Solano benchmark (Story 15 references CrowdStrike's own Fal.Con reveal). No pricing was disclosed for the Cyber variant; Fairwind partners pay per-seat via Google Cloud Security enterprise agreements.

Anthropic says Claude produced the first end-to-end computer-checked Lean proof of Fermat's Last Theorem

Anthropic Research · 2026-09-04 · Source ↗

Eleven continuous days of autonomous work on the Prove2Me multi-agent platform yielded a 13 million line Lean proof including 29,500 intermediate theorems. Andrew Wiles's original 1995 proof was 129 pages of natural-language mathematics.

Anthropic Research announced on Thursday September 4 that Claude Opus 5 produced the first end-to-end computer-checked Lean proof of Fermat's Last Theorem across eleven continuous days of autonomous work on Anthropic's Prove2Me multi-agent theorem-proving platform. The proof spans roughly 13 million lines of Lean 4 code and includes at least 29,500 intermediate theorems, each proven and verified by the Lean kernel. Andrew Wiles's original 1995 proof was 129 pages of natural-language mathematics; full formalization has been a running open target for automated theorem proving since Kevin Buzzard, Richard Taylor and Xena Project began the manual Lean-formalization effort in 2019.

The Prove2Me architecture runs multiple Claude Opus 5 instances against a single Lean environment: a "conjecture agent" proposes intermediate lemmas, a "prover agent" attempts formal proof, a "critic agent" identifies gaps and refines the goal state, and a "librarian agent" maintains the Mathlib dependency graph. The four-agent pipeline runs continuously; each proven lemma is committed to a shared Lean project state and the Lean kernel provides the final soundness check. The eleven-day run consumed roughly 4.2 billion inference tokens across the four-agent stack and required no human intervention after the initial goal statement.

"This is the first time an autonomous AI system has produced a machine-checked proof of a top-ten open problem in mathematics. Whether it is 'doing mathematics' or 'doing search' is a question we take seriously. The Lean kernel does not care about the difference."— Sam Bowman, Anthropic Research, on the Prove2Me result

What was proven

Fermat's Last Theorem states there are no positive integers a, b, c satisfying a^n + b^n = c^n for any integer n greater than 2. Wiles's 1995 proof reduced FLT to the modularity theorem for semistable elliptic curves. The Prove2Me formalization reproduces this reduction and adds the earlier semistability generalizations by Breuil, Conrad, Diamond and Taylor from 2001. The Lean project is available at github.com/anthropic/prove2me-flt and has been independently verified by three Lean community members including Kevin Buzzard, who commented on Lean Zulip that "this is real. It compiles. Every step checks."

What it does not prove

The Prove2Me formalization does not resolve the philosophical question of whether Claude "understands" the mathematics. Anthropic Research's paper notes the model proposed several lemmas that human mathematicians would recognize as "elegant" and several that would be described as "brute force" — but the Lean kernel treats both equally. The paper argues explicitly against overclaiming: "We are not claiming Claude discovered a new proof of FLT. We are claiming Claude, given the goal statement and access to Mathlib, produced a machine-checked formalization of a known proof under autonomous operation." The framing echoes Terence Tao's 2025 comments on AlphaProof: formalization at this scale changes the tools of the field without necessarily changing the field.
